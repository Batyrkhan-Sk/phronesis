Some stories hang on a single object. The identity of the object turns out not to matter.

## What it is

A **MacGuffin** is whatever the characters are chasing: stolen plans, a secret formula, a briefcase, a statuette. Alfred Hitchcock said what matters is that it seems **vitally important to the characters**. What it actually is, he suggested, can be almost anything. The audience needs the characters’ urgency, not the details.

Hitchcock’s own films give many examples. The engine plans in *The 39 Steps* and the uranium in *Notorious* are commonly cited. In *Psycho*, the $40,000 that Marion Crane steals starts everything, then fades once the story takes over.

## The lion trap

Hitchcock explained the idea with a joke. Two men sit on a train, and one asks what the parcel in the luggage rack is. “A MacGuffin,” says the other, “for trapping lions in the Scottish Highlands.” There are no lions in the Highlands. “Then that’s no MacGuffin,” says the first. The point of the joke is that a MacGuffin is nothing at all, and the story is about the people chasing it.

## Where the word came from

The earliest Oxford English Dictionary citation is from a 1939 lecture Hitchcock gave at Columbia University. Hitchcock said he believed a scenario editor called **Angus MacPhail**, from his Gaumont-British days, coined it. That is his recollection, and writers have long debated how far the story reaches back. Whether the train joke predates Hitchcock is **not established**.

## A useful lens

Once you know the word, you start noticing it: the ring in a fantasy epic, the briefcase in a thriller. It also separates two tools that people confuse. Chekhov’s gun must **pay off**. A MacGuffin only has to **get people moving**.

Next time a film’s plot is ridiculed for resting on a vague object, you can ask the gentler question: what is the story really about?
