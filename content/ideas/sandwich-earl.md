Nobody invented putting food between bread. People across the world had done it for centuries. What **John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich** (1718–1792) provided was the **name**.

## The gambling story

The famous account comes from the French writer **Pierre-Jean Grosley**, who spent a year in London in 1765 and published *Londres* in 1770. He reported that a minister of state spent long hours at the gaming table and called for **salt beef between two slices of toast**, so he could eat without stopping play or getting grease on his cards.

It became the standard origin story, and it fitted the earl’s reputation as a rake.

## The desk alternative

Sandwich was also a hard-working politician: **First Lord of the Admiralty** three times, and a patron of Captain Cook (who named the Sandwich Islands, now Hawaii, after him). His biographer **N. A. M. Rodger** argues that, given his workload, he was **more likely eating at his desk**. Grosley’s story reads like London gossip.

Either way, the name caught on among fashionable Londoners: ordering “the same as Sandwich” became ordering “a sandwich”. It may be the most widely used aristocratic title in the world.
