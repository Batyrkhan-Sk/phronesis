When a printer set a page by hand, the letters were **pieces of metal**, one per character, kept in a wooden tray divided into small compartments. The tray was called a **case**. The printer, called a **compositor**, stood at it, picked the letters one by one and lined them up in a small hand-held tool.

## Two cases, one above the other

Capital letters were used less often than small ones. So the compositor kept them in a separate case, **tilted up on the rack above**, and the everyday small letters in the case below, within easy reach. They became the **upper case** and the **lower case**. In his 1683 printing manual, Joseph Moxon refers to both, and the Oxford English Dictionary cites him as the earliest known use.

## What the words really mean

Many people assume “upper” and “lower” describe the letters’ **height**, or that capitals are somehow higher in status. Neither is true: the words describe a **shelf**. Over time the phrase moved from the printing house into the language, and then onto computer keyboards as “shift” and “caps lock”.

## A caveat

“The upper case” was not one fixed arrangement. Layouts differed between countries and workshops. Later, printers in America often used a single **job case** with capitals and small letters together, but the names stuck anyway, as they did in typesetting software.

Next time you press shift, you’re doing what a compositor did when reaching for the upper tray.
