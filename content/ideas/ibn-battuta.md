In **1325**, **Ibn Battuta**, a young legal scholar from **Tangier** in Morocco, left home for the pilgrimage to **Mecca**, a journey of about 16 months. He didn’t come home for **29 years**.

## Where he went

His travels took him through:

- North Africa, Egypt, Syria and Arabia
- the Swahili coast of **East Africa**
- **Anatolia**, the steppes of the Golden Horde and **Central Asia** (Bukhara, Samarkand)
- **India**, where he served the Sultan of Delhi as a judge for years
- the **Maldives**, where he was again a judge, and married
- **Sri Lanka**, **Sumatra**, and probably **China**
- and finally **al-Andalus** in Spain and across the Sahara to **Mali**

The estimated total is about **117,000 km**. By comparison, estimates for **Zheng He** are around 50,000 km and for **Marco Polo** around 24,000 km.

## How it worked

The Islamic world of the 14th century was vast and connected. A trained jurist who spoke Arabic could find work, hospitality and patrons from Morocco to India. Ibn Battuta’s legal training was his passport.

## Did he really go everywhere?

Back in Morocco he **dictated** his memories to the scholar **Ibn Juzayy**, producing the **Rihla** (“The Journey”). Some passages, especially about **China** and the city of **Bulgar** on the Volga, are vague or borrowed from earlier writers, and historians doubt he went to all of these places. Even with those parts set aside, it remains one of the great travel accounts of the medieval world.
