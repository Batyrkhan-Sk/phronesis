**Hedy Lamarr** was one of MGM’s biggest stars of the 1940s, often billed as “the most beautiful woman in the world”. Away from the set, she liked inventing things.

## The problem

Early in World War II, radio-guided torpedoes had a weakness: the enemy could **jam** the single frequency that controlled them. Lamarr realised that if the transmitter and the torpedo **hopped between frequencies** in a shared secret pattern, jamming would fail.

## The piano connection

Her collaborator **George Antheil**, an avant-garde composer, had once synchronised several **player pianos**, which are driven by punched paper rolls. They proposed using a similar mechanism, miniaturised, to keep transmitter and receiver hopping in step.

**US patent 2,292,387** was granted on **11 August 1942**, under her married name, Hedy Kiesler Markey.

## What happened next

The Navy **rejected** it as too bulky to fit in a torpedo. Operational systems using similar ideas appeared only in the 1960s, after the patent had expired, and Lamarr earned nothing from it.

Popular accounts sometimes say she “invented Wi-Fi” or “invented frequency hopping”. Neither is true: the concept existed earlier. Her patent was a clever, specific design. Recognition came late: the **EFF Pioneer Award** in 1997 and the **National Inventors Hall of Fame** in 2014, after her death.
