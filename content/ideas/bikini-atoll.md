When people say something “went off like a bomb”, they usually don’t mean it literally. In 1946 a French engineer meant it very nearly so.

## A week after the bomb

In the summer of 1946 the United States carried out nuclear tests at **Bikini Atoll** in the Pacific, and the news was everywhere. On **5 July 1946**, at the **Piscine Molitor**, a popular Paris swimming pool, **Louis Réard** showed a new two-piece swimsuit and called it the **bikini**. The name was a marketing bet: he hoped the suit would cause an explosion of its own.

## Why a dancer modelled it

Réard struggled to find a professional model willing to wear it. He hired **Micheline Bernardini**, an 18-year-old dancer at the Casino de Paris, who modelled it. The design was reportedly a tiny top and two triangles of cloth held by string, and what shocked people most was that it showed the navel.

## Not the only “atomic” swimsuit

Réard had a rival. **Jacques Heim** sold a scaled-down two-piece that still covered the navel, called the **Atome** (French for atom), and advertised it as the world’s smallest bathing suit. Réard’s answer, according to widely repeated accounts, was that his was smaller still. The atom-themed names show how thoroughly the new nuclear age had seeped into everyday language, even swimwear. Sources disagree on some of the details of Heim’s timeline, so keep that part loose.

## Scandal, then standard

Several countries restricted the bikini, and the Vatican condemned it, although accounts vary on exactly when and where. Yet within a generation it had become ordinary beach wear.
