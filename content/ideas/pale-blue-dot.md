By 1990, **Voyager 1** had finished its tour of the planets and was leaving the solar system. Its cameras would soon be turned off for good to save power.

**Carl Sagan**, who worked on the imaging team, had argued for years for one last picture: turn around and photograph **home**. There was resistance. The image had no scientific value, and pointing the camera close to the Sun risked damaging it. On **14 February 1990** the spacecraft took a series of 60 frames forming a “Family Portrait” of the solar system.

In one frame, Earth appears as a **bluish-white speck** occupying about **0.12 of a pixel**, sitting by chance in a streak of scattered sunlight.

## What Sagan made of it

In his 1994 book Sagan wrote that everyone you love, everyone you have ever heard of, “every king and peasant… every saint and sinner” lived “on a mote of dust suspended in a sunbeam.”

The passage is often quoted as an argument for **humility** and for **taking care of each other**: there is nowhere else, at least for now, to go.

Astronauts describe a related shift in perspective after seeing Earth from orbit, called the **overview effect**. Most of us will only ever get it second-hand, through images like this one.
