Next time you put on blue jeans, you are wearing two European cities.

## Genoa gave us “jeans”

**Jeans** most likely comes from **Gênes**, the French name for **Genoa**. The Italian port was linked with a hard-wearing cotton cloth, often associated with sailors, and the English word for the cloth and then the trousers grew out of that. The phrase “blue jeans” is sometimes explained as a distortion of the French *bleu de Gênes*, “blue of Genoa”.

## Nîmes gave us “denim”

**Denim** most likely comes from **serge de Nîmes**, a twilled cloth associated with the French city of Nîmes. One of the earliest recorded English uses, “serge denim”, is from **1695**. There is a caveat worth knowing: the older Nîmes cloth may have contained wool or silk, so it was not necessarily the same fabric as today’s cotton denim. The name travelled even if the recipe changed.

## The rivets

The modern blue jean needs a third person. **Jacob Davis**, a tailor in Reno, Nevada, began putting metal rivets at the weak points of work trousers, such as pocket corners. He could not afford the patent fee alone, so he wrote to his fabric supplier, **Levi Strauss** of San Francisco. They were granted **US patent No. 139,121** in **May 1873**.

A tidy story says Strauss invented jeans single-handedly, or started with brown canvas trousers and then switched to denim. Historians treat that as a myth: the patent belongs to both men, and the idea of the rivet was Davis’s.

## Why tell it

It is a small example of how clothes, like words, carry routes of trade inside them. It also corrects a popular legend in a friendly way: the person who is famous for something is not always the person who thought of it.
