Think of a scene where a character realises something terrible. The face stays steady on screen, but the corridor behind them seems to stretch away, or the walls close in. That is a **dolly zoom**.

## How it works

Two things happen at once. The camera **dollies**, meaning it physically moves toward or away from the subject. And the lens **zooms** in the opposite direction. If the camera backs away while zooming in, the person stays the same size in the frame, but the background looks as if it is being pulled closer and flattened. If the camera moves in while zooming out, the background seems to stretch away.

The effect works because the two movements change the size of the subject in opposite ways, and cancel, while the background is affected only by the change in perspective. Your eye is expecting the whole picture to behave as one, and it doesn’t.

## Vertigo

It is most famously associated with Alfred Hitchcock’s *Vertigo* (1958), where it conveys the main character’s fear of heights as he looks down a stairwell. Wikipedia credits **Irmin Roberts**, a second-unit cameraman at Paramount, with working out how to do it for Hitchcock. Roberts was reportedly not credited in the film. That is why you will see it called the **Vertigo effect** or the **Hitchcock zoom**.

Whether *Vertigo* was truly the first use is argued about: discussion on Wikipedia mentions earlier claims, including a shot in Hitchcock’s own *Spellbound*. So it is safest to say that *Vertigo* made the shot famous.

## Jaws and after

In *Jaws* (1975) the technique is used on the police chief’s face on the beach as the shark attacks, and it is sometimes called the **Jaws effect**. Many directors since have used it for moments of shock, realisation or panic.

## Spot it yourself

Once you know about it, you start noticing it. A good test: next time a character’s world seems to tilt around a still face, ask which way the camera moved and which way the lens did.
