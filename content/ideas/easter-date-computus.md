Christmas is always 25 December. Easter wanders across five weeks of the spring calendar. The reason is a rule that is half astronomy and half bookkeeping.

## The rule

In Western churches, Easter is the **first Sunday after the first full moon on or after 21 March**. Early Christians wanted it to follow the Jewish Passover season, which is tied to the spring moon, but also to land on a Sunday.

Working this out for any given year is called **computus**, Latin for “calculation”. It was a serious craft in medieval monasteries, where monks needed to know the date years ahead.

## A moon that isn’t the moon

Calculating from real astronomy was hard, so the church used tables. The result is the **ecclesiastical full moon**: a date fixed by a repeating cycle, not by observation. It usually matches the real full moon, but can be off by a day or two.

The equinox is treated the same way. Churches simply fix it at **21 March**, though the actual equinox can fall on 19, 20 or 21 March. The earliest possible Easter is **22 March** and the latest is **25 April**, giving 35 possible dates.

## What Nicaea did and didn’t do

It is often said that the **Council of Nicaea** in 325 set the date of Easter. It addressed the question and pushed for one common practice, rather than leaving communities to follow different customs. But the detailed calendar rules were worked out in the centuries afterwards, and not by that council. Treat “Nicaea fixed the formula” as an oversimplification.

## Two Easters

Most Eastern Orthodox churches apply the same rule using the older **Julian calendar**, so their Easter usually falls later than the Western one. In some years the two coincide. It is the same feast and the same rule, counted on different calendars, just as with Christmas on 7 January.

## The connection to the calendar you use

The year numbering of **Anno Domini** was devised by a monk preparing Easter tables, so the way the Western world counts years comes from the same calculation. Easter has quietly shaped how we measure time.
