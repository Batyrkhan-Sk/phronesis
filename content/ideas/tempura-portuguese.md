In **1543** a ship carrying Portuguese sailors landed on the Japanese island of Tanegashima, the first contact between Japan and Europe. Traders and **Jesuit missionaries** followed, and the port of **Nagasaki** became their base.

They brought guns, Christianity, bread and a cooking technique: dipping food in **batter** and **deep-frying** it.

## Little fish from the garden

A likely ancestor of tempura is the Portuguese **peixinhos da horta**, “little fish from the garden”: green beans dipped in batter and fried until they look like small fried fish. It was a dish for days when Catholics abstained from meat.

## The name

The most widely accepted theory says **tempura** comes from the Latin **tempora**, “times”, as in the *quatuor anni tempora*, the **Ember Days**: seasonal fasting days when Catholics ate fish and vegetables instead of meat. An alternative links it to Portuguese *tempero*, “seasoning”. The Latin explanation is better supported.

## Becoming Japanese

During the **Edo period** the batter was simplified to flour, egg and cold water, and tempura became a popular **street food** in Edo (today’s Tokyo), sold from stalls alongside sushi and soba. The technique was foreign, but the light, crisp result is entirely Japanese.
