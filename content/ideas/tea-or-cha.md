Ask for tea around the world and you’ll mostly hear one of two words.

| “Cha” family | “Te” family |
| --- | --- |
| Hindi **chai** | English **tea** |
| Russian **chay** | French **thé** |
| Persian **chāy** | German **Tee** |
| Turkish **çay** | Dutch **thee** |
| Portuguese **chá** | Malay **teh** |

Both come from **Chinese**, which is written with the same character, 茶. The difference is **which Chinese** and **which route**.

## Cha: by land, and by Portuguese ship

**Cha** is the pronunciation in Mandarin and Cantonese. It travelled west along the **Silk Road** and other overland routes, into Persian, then into Hindi, Russian, Turkish and Arabic. The **Portuguese**, who traded through **Macau** near Canton, took **chá** home too.

## Te: by Dutch ship

On the coast of **Fujian**, people spoke **Min Chinese**, in which the word was **te**. In the 17th century the **Dutch** dominated the sea trade in tea from Fujian ports, and their **thee** spread to most of western Europe, including English **tea**.

In the World Atlas of Language Structures sample, **110** languages use a *cha* word, **84** a *te* word, and **36** neither.

## The exceptions

In the very popular Hacker News discussion of this map, commenters quickly pointed out exceptions. **Polish** says **herbata**, from Latin *herba thea*, “tea herb”. And in some tea-growing regions, local languages use their own words. The rule is a strong tendency, not a law. It still tells you a lot about trade history through one everyday word.
