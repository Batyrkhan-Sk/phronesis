In **1770**, the Hungarian inventor **Wolfgang von Kempelen** presented a marvel to **Empress Maria Theresa** in Vienna: a life-sized figure in Ottoman robes seated at a cabinet, which **played chess**, and played it well.

Before each game, the operator opened the cabinet doors to show gears and clockwork. Then the figure moved its arm, picked up pieces and beat most challengers.

## The secret

Hidden inside was a **skilled human chess player**, who slid about to stay out of view when the doors were opened, followed the game through magnets under the board, and controlled the arm with levers. The machinery on display was mostly for show.

## A long tour

The Turk toured Europe and America for **decades**:

- **Benjamin Franklin** played it, and kept a book about it in his library.
- **Napoleon** is said to have played it in 1809, trying illegal moves until the Turk swept the pieces off the board, but the versions of the story vary and it is probably exaggerated.
- In **1836**, **Edgar Allan Poe** published “Maelzel’s Chess-Player”, arguing that it must contain a person. Many of his specific guesses were wrong, but the conclusion was right.

The Turk was **destroyed by fire** in Philadelphia on **5 July 1854**.

## Its name lives on

In 2005 **Amazon** launched **Amazon Mechanical Turk**, a marketplace where people are paid to do small tasks that computers can’t yet handle, such as labelling images and transcribing text. Much of the data used to train modern AI has been labelled this way. It’s an honest name: behind the apparent automation, there are people.
