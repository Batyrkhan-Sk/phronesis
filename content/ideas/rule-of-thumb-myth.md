Many people “know” where **rule of thumb** comes from: an old English law that let a man beat his wife, so long as the stick was no thicker than his thumb. It is a vivid story, and it is not true.

## What the phrase actually means

A rule of thumb is a rough guide, learned from experience rather than calculated. It appears in English in the 17th century, in contexts such as craftsmen judging by eye and hand instead of by precise measure. The thumb was a handy measuring tool: its width was roughly an inch, and many languages have a word for ‘thumb’ that also means ‘inch’. That is the most likely reason the phrase uses it, though nobody can prove it.

## Where the legend came from

The story is usually pinned on **Sir Francis Buller**, an 18th-century English judge, who was said to have ruled that a husband could beat his wife with a stick no thicker than his thumb. In 1782 or 1783 (sources differ) the caricaturist **James Gillray** published a print mocking him as “Judge Thumb”. The Victorian biographer Edward Foss searched for evidence in 1870 and found none that Buller had ever said it. The satire seems to show his reputation for harshness, not a real ruling.

Real law of the time did give husbands troubling powers over their wives, which is partly why the story feels plausible. But the thumb rule itself is not in any statute or ruling anyone has found.

## How a myth caught on

The link between the phrase and domestic violence took off in the 1970s and was boosted in 1982, when the US Commission on Civil Rights titled a report on battered women *Under the Rule of Thumb*. The report used the legend as a figure of speech, and many readers took it as history.

## A kind thing to bring to dinner

If someone repeats the story, you needn’t lecture them. You can say that it is an understandable mistake, that the real history of how the law treated women was bad enough without the legend, and that the phrase is much more humble: it means ‘a guess your hands have learned’.
