“Breakfast, lunch and dinner” feels like a law of nature. It isn’t.

## The two-meal day

In his book *Millennium*, the historian **Ian Mortimer** describes northern Europe around **1500**: most people ate **dinner**, the main meal, at around **11 a.m.**, and **supper** at around **5 p.m.** Few people ate breakfast. Labourers, children, the sick and travellers sometimes had something early, but it wasn’t a fixed meal for everyone.

## What changed

- **Town work.** As more people worked long hours for employers in towns, they couldn’t stop for supper at five. Supper moved later into the evening.
- **Dinner slipped.** The main meal moved a couple of hours later and became what we’d call **lunch**.
- **A gap appeared.** Getting from waking up to a later lunch required food: **breakfast**.
- **School.** More boys went to school, and long lessons required them to eat first.

By **1600**, Mortimer argues, breakfast was normal in towns.

## The word itself

The word records the change. *Breakfast* is “**breaking the fast**” of the night. And the meaning of **dinner** has slid from midday to evening over the centuries, which is why some English speakers still call their midday meal “dinner” and their evening meal “tea”.

This question has been popular on History Stack Exchange, where people generally agree that meal times followed **work** much more than any idea about health.
