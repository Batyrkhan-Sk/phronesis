The Moon always shows us the same face. That is not because it doesn’t spin: it spins exactly once for every orbit around Earth, which is why we never see the other side.

## Why the same face?

Earth’s gravity raised tides in the young Moon and slowly braked its rotation until the two motions matched. Astronomers call this **tidal locking** (or captured rotation). It is common: many moons in the Solar System are locked to their planets.

## Far, not dark

The hemisphere we cannot see is the **far side**. It has day and night like everywhere else on the Moon, with a long lunar day of about two weeks of sunlight. When the Moon is new and looks black from Earth, it is the *near* side that is in darkness and the far side that is sunlit.

“Dark” in “dark side” was used in the older sense of **unknown**, as in the “Dark Continent”. The name stuck, and later became a very good album title.

## A little more than half

The Moon also wobbles slightly as it orbits, an effect called **libration**. It lets us peek around the edges, so over time about **59%** of the surface is visible from Earth and roughly 41% never is.

## First look

Until 7 October 1959, nobody had seen the far side. The Soviet probe **Luna 3** photographed it and sent the pictures back to Earth. They were grainy, but they showed a surface with far fewer dark lava plains than the face we know. Accounts differ on how many frames were usable, so it is safest to say simply that it produced the first images.

## Why look different?

The two hemispheres differ noticeably: the near side has large dark “seas” of ancient lava, while the far side is mostly bright, cratered highland. Why this is so is still being researched, and it is better described as an open question than a settled one.
