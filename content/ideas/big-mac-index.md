**Purchasing power parity** is the idea that, in the long run, exchange rates should adjust so that the same basket of goods costs the same everywhere. Measuring a real basket across countries is hard. In **1986**, *The Economist* journalist **Pam Woodall** suggested a basket of exactly one item: a Big Mac.

## How to read it

If a Big Mac costs $5.50 in the US and the equivalent of $3.50 in another country, the burger suggests that country’s currency is **undervalued** against the dollar by about a third. If it costs $7, the currency looks **overvalued**.

## Why it’s imperfect, and still useful

- A burger includes **local costs** such as rent and wages, which are naturally cheaper in poorer countries. *The Economist* now publishes a GDP-adjusted version.
- Taxes, competition and McDonald’s pricing strategy vary by country.
- In some places a Big Mac is a cheap meal; in others it is a treat.

The index lasts because it is **concrete**. Most people will never read a purchasing-power paper, but anyone can compare burger prices on holiday. Explaining an abstract idea through a familiar object is a good way to make it stick.
