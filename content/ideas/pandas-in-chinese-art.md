Look through three thousand years of Chinese art: Shang bronzes, Tang poems, Song landscape paintings, Ming novels, Qing porcelain. You’ll find **dragons, phoenixes, tigers, cranes, carp**, even **cicadas and crickets**. You will struggle to find a **giant panda**.

## Why the panda was missing

The historian **E. Elena Songster**, in *Panda Nation* (2018), gives two reasons:

1. **No symbolism.** Traditional Chinese art usually showed animals for what they *meant*. The crane stood for long life and the carp for perseverance, often because of puns on their names. The panda carried no such meaning.
2. **Nobody saw them.** Giant pandas are solitary and live in remote mountain forests. Few people ever met one. The handful of possible references in old texts appear in **medical compendiums**, and were forgotten in modern times.

## How it became China’s animal

In **1869**, the French missionary and naturalist **Armand David** sent a panda skin to Paris and introduced the species to Western science. Western hunters and zoos followed in the early 20th century. In the second half of the century, China made the panda a symbol of **conservation** and of the nation, and used it in **diplomacy**.

Songster notes that pandas began appearing in “traditional” Chinese art only within the last few decades. Some national symbols are very old; this one is surprisingly new.

The question was popular on History Stack Exchange, and the accepted answer makes a point worth remembering: an animal’s absence from art says more about **what artists valued** than about whether the animal existed.
