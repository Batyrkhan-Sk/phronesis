Imagine you are walking down a country road and find a **fence** built across it. You can see no reason for it. Surely, you think, it should go.

The writer **G. K. Chesterton** put this scene into his 1929 book ***The Thing***, in a chapter called “The Drift from Domesticity”. He opens with what he calls a simple principle, one that will “probably be called a paradox”. Faced with the reformer who says he cannot see the use of the fence, the sensible reply is: **go away and think**. Only when you can come back and say what it was for may you be trusted to remove it.

## The point is not “never change anything”

This is the most common misreading. Chesterton was talking about **reforming** things, “as distinct from deforming them”. Nothing in the principle says the fence must stay. It says that removing it is a decision to make **after** you understand it, not before. Sometimes you will discover the reason is gone and tear it down with a clear conscience. Sometimes you will discover it was holding back a bull.

The name “Chesterton’s fence” is later shorthand. Chesterton never used it himself, but it caught on and is now used in programming, law, medicine and politics. Justice Neil Gorsuch even opened and closed a 2018 dissent with the image.

## Using it kindly

It makes a lovely habit at dinner and at work:

- **Ask what a rule was solving** before laughing at it: an odd office process, a family tradition, a strange line in a contract.
- **Make the case for the thing** you want to remove. If you can’t, you may not yet know enough to remove it.
- **Treat the answer “nobody knows” as information**, not permission. It means you need to look harder.

It is also a good-natured way to disagree. Instead of “that’s pointless”, you can say “I wonder what that was for.”
