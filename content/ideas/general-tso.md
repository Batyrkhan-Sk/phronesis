**Zuo Zongtang** (1812–1885) was a formidable general of the late Qing dynasty, from **Hunan** province. He helped crush the Taiping Rebellion and reconquered Xinjiang. There is no record of him eating anything like the chicken named after him.

## Invented in Taiwan

After the Chinese Civil War, the Hunanese chef **Peng Chang-kuei** fled with the Nationalist government to **Taiwan**. In Taipei, around the **1950s**, he created a dish of fried chicken in a **hot, sour** Hunan-style sauce, and named it after his province’s most famous general. In the documentary *The Search for General Tso* (2014) he recalls cooking it for a state banquet in 1952.

## Sweetened in New York

In **1973** Peng opened **Uncle Peng’s Hunan Yuan** in Manhattan. Around the same time **T. T. Wang** at Shun Lee Palace served a crispy version called **General Ching’s chicken**. American diners loved it, and the sauce became **sweeter** and the chicken **crispier**. Copies spread to takeaways across North America.

## Rejected at home

When Peng later opened a restaurant in **Hunan** serving the dish, local diners found it **too sweet**, and the restaurant soon closed. Peng died in Taipei in 2016, aged 98.

The writer Jennifer 8. Lee, who also traced the fortune cookie, uses General Tso’s chicken as her favourite example of American Chinese food. It is a cuisine in its own right, invented by immigrants for a new audience, and no less real for not being “authentic”.
