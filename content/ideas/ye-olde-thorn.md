Old English writers had two letters for the “th” sound: **þ** (thorn) and **ð** (eth). Eth disappeared early. Thorn survived into the age of printing.

## How þ turned into y

Two things happened at once:

1. In late medieval handwriting, the shape of **þ** became less distinctive and started to look like a **y**.
2. When printing reached England in the 1470s, much of the type came from **Belgium and the Netherlands**, where nobody used þ. Printers substituted the closest-looking letter: **y**.

So **þe** (“the”) was printed as **ye**, often with a small *e* written above the *y*. Readers knew it meant *the*.

## Ye Olde confusion

Once þ had been forgotten, people began reading the Y literally. That gave us the mock-medieval “**Ye Olde Tea Shoppe**”, read as “yee oldy”. It was never pronounced that way.

(Confusingly, English also had a real word **ye**, meaning “you”, as in “hear ye”. That one *was* pronounced with a y. It is a different word.)

## Where thorn survives

**Icelandic** still uses þ every day: the country’s parliament, for example, is the **Alþingi**.
