Ask where tulips come from and most people say the **Netherlands**. The Dutch made them famous, but tulips are wild plants of **Central Asia**. The centre of their diversity is the **Pamir-Alai and Tian Shan** mountains, a region that includes southern **Kazakhstan**, where wild species such as *Tulipa greigii* and *Tulipa alberti* bloom across the hills each spring.

## The road west

Tulips were cultivated and loved in the **Ottoman Empire**, where they became a symbol of luxury and appeared in art and textiles. From there they reached Western Europe. The Flemish botanist **Carolus Clusius** grew them in Vienna in 1573 and, after moving to **Leiden** University, planted them there. They first flowered in the Netherlands in **1594**.

## Mania

Within forty years, rare tulip bulbs, especially “broken” ones with flame-like streaks of colour, were selling for extraordinary sums. Between **1634 and 1637** the trade became **tulip mania**, often called the **first recorded speculative bubble**, before prices collapsed in early 1637.

The streaks, it later turned out, were caused by a **virus** that weakened the bulbs.

## How bad was it?

The traditional story says tulip mania ruined thousands and devastated the Dutch economy. The historian **Anne Goldgar**, who studied the archival records, argues that this was **greatly exaggerated**. Relatively few people were involved and few were ruined, and much of the drama came from moralising pamphlets afterwards. The bubble was real; the catastrophe is debated.

Next time you see a field of Dutch tulips, it’s worth remembering they started out as wild flowers on Kazakh mountainsides.
