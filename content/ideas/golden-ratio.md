Split a line so that **the whole relates to the longer part as the longer part relates to the shorter**, and the ratio is

> φ = (1 + √5) ÷ 2 ≈ **1.6180339…**

## What is true

- φ is built into the **regular pentagon** and the five-pointed star.
- Divide consecutive [Fibonacci numbers](idea:fibonacci) (21/13, 34/21, 55/34…) and you approach φ.
- Many plants place each new seed or leaf about **137.5° (the “golden angle”)** around from the last. This packs seeds efficiently and produces the spirals in sunflowers and pinecones.

## What is myth

- **The Parthenon**: claims rely on choosing convenient measurement points; the building does not fit φ well.
- **The Mona Lisa and Leonardo’s paintings**: there is no evidence Leonardo composed them around φ.
- **The “most beautiful rectangle”**: experiments since the 1800s give mixed results, and people’s preferences vary widely.

## Why the myth is so durable

Measure almost anything complicated, such as a face, a building or a logo, and with enough freedom about **where** to measure you will find ratios near 1.6. Numbers between 1.5 and 1.7 are everywhere. The mathematician George Markowsky called this out in a 1992 paper that remains the standard debunking.

The real history is still interesting. Luca Pacioli’s *De divina proportione* (1509), illustrated by **Leonardo da Vinci**, celebrated the ratio as divine. It was mainly a book of geometry, especially polyhedra, rather than a manual of aesthetics. The idea of a “golden” ratio as the key to beauty was largely popularised in the **19th century** by Adolf Zeising.

**A useful takeaway:** when a pattern is claimed to be everywhere, ask how much freedom the person had to look for it.
