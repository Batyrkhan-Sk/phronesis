In **1955**, a young mathematician at Dartmouth College, **John McCarthy**, joined three others to write a funding proposal: **Marvin Minsky** of Harvard, **Nathaniel Rochester** of IBM, and **Claude Shannon** of Bell Labs, the founder of information theory.

They proposed “a **2-month, 10-man study of artificial intelligence** … during the summer of 1956 at Dartmouth College.”

## The name

That proposal is where the phrase **artificial intelligence** comes from. McCarthy chose it partly for its **neutrality**, to avoid narrow “automata theory” and the then-fashionable **cybernetics**, a field built on analogue feedback and control.

## The confidence

The proposal’s central conjecture was that “every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it.” And it predicted that “a significant advance can be made … if a carefully selected group of scientists work on it together **for a summer**.”

## What actually happened

The workshop ran for roughly **six to eight weeks** in summer **1956**, funded by the **Rockefeller Foundation**. People came and went. Among them were **Allen Newell** and **Herbert Simon**, who brought the **Logic Theorist**, a program that proved theorems from Russell and Whitehead’s *Principia Mathematica*.

No single breakthrough came out of that summer. But the workshop named the field and gathered the people who would lead it for the next two decades, and it set the tone of confident, ambitious prediction that would later cause trouble.
