Ask a Kazakh grandparent about family and you may get a recitation: a father, his father, his father, and on, **seven generations** back. This is **jeti ata**, literally “seven fathers”, and children were traditionally taught it from an early age.

## The rule behind it

The point wasn’t only pride in ancestry. Among Kazakhs, people who share a paternal ancestor **within seven generations** are not supposed to marry. Before a wedding, families would check that the bride’s and groom’s lines did not meet within that range. Without written records, the only way to apply the rule was for everyone to **remember**.

The tradition is often explained as a way to avoid the harm of marriages between close relatives. Whether that was its conscious purpose or a later explanation is hard to say, but the effect is the same: a wide circle of exogamy, larger than most legal systems require.

## A living genealogy

Jeti ata is the core of a much bigger tradition, the **shezhire**: genealogies linking families to clans, clans to tribes, and tribes to the three zhuz. For a nomadic people without fixed villages, genealogy did the work that an address does for others. When two strangers met on the steppe, asking “who are your ancestors?” quickly showed whether they were related, and how.

The same custom is found among **Kyrgyz** and **Bashkirs**. Today many Kazakh families still keep their shezhire, some in printed books and online family trees, and it is still common to hear someone name their seven fathers at a family gathering.
