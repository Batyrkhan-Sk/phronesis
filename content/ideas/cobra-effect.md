## The legend

The story goes that colonial officials in Delhi, worried about venomous snakes, offered a reward for every **dead cobra**. Enterprising locals began **breeding cobras** for the bounty. When the government cancelled the scheme, the breeders released their now-worthless snakes, and there were more cobras than before.

It is a perfect parable, and **there is no solid historical evidence for it**. The economist Horst Siebert popularised it in 2001 under the name *Der Kobra-Effekt*.

## The documented version

In **1902**, French colonial Hanoi was building modern sewers, which turned out to be ideal for rats, and plague was a threat. The administration paid a bounty for each rat, and to save the officials from handling corpses, people only had to hand in **the tail**.

The historian **Michael Vann** found the results in the archives:
- **Tailless rats** turned up around the city: people cut off the tails and released the rats to breed.
- Rats were **smuggled in** from outside the city.
- Inspectors found **rat farms** on the outskirts.

## The lesson

Rewards change behaviour, often not in the way intended. Before designing an incentive, ask: **what is the cheapest way to earn this reward without solving the problem?** Someone will find it.
