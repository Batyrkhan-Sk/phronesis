Play a rising scale on a piano and it ends up higher. Play a **Shepard scale** and it seems to rise, and rise, and rise, yet after twelve steps you are back exactly where you began.

## How it works

Pitch has two qualities: which note it is (a C, say), and which **octave** it is in. A Shepard tone separates the two.

Each note is made of several sine waves, each an octave apart. As the scale steps up, every component rises by a step. The lowest components fade in, from silence, while the highest fade out into silence. Your ear follows the rising motion of each part, but the overall mix never gets higher, because the top is always disappearing and a new bottom is always arriving.

The **psychologist Roger Shepard** described it in 1964, in a paper on “circularity” in pitch judgement. The composer **Jean-Claude Risset** later made a smooth, gliding version, the **Shepard–Risset glissando**, which seems to slide up for ever.

## An auditory staircase

People often compare it to the Penrose stairs, the impossible staircase that always climbs but loops back on itself. That is a useful picture, but it is only an analogy.

## Why film makers love it

An endless rise gives you tension without a release. The score of *Dunkirk* (2017) is built on this principle, and Christopher Nolan has also described shaping the film’s three storylines, on land, sea and air, so that intensity keeps climbing in the same way. Zimmer and Nolan have talked about using it in other films too. Next time a soundtrack makes you feel an uneasy, tightening climb that never quite peaks, you may be hearing it.

## Try it yourself

Many free recordings of the Shepard scale and the Risset glissando exist online. Listen for the moment you realise the “higher” note is the same one you heard earlier. It is a lovely thing to play for a friend: it is surprising, harmless, and it explains something real about how hearing works.
