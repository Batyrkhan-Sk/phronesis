Try writing a paragraph without the letter **e**. It is the most common letter in French and in English, so the ban hits almost every sentence. In 1969 the French writer **Georges Perec** did it for a whole novel, **La Disparition**, of roughly 300 pages.

## A rule as a starting point

Perec belonged to **Oulipo**, a French group of writers and mathematicians who held that a strict rule can free a writer rather than trap them. A text that avoids a chosen letter is called a **lipogram**. Perec’s was the grand version.

The book is also a mystery, and what it is about is disappearance. People vanish, and the characters keep failing to name what is missing. Readers often take the absent letter as a deliberate echo of loss. That is an interpretation, not something the author spelled out in a single sentence, but the fit is hard to ignore.

## Translating the untranslatable

Translating it looks absurd: you would have to keep the plot and the rule. **Gilbert Adair** did exactly that in **A Void**, published in the mid-1990s. Because the French and English words differ, he could not translate line by line. He rebuilt passages so that the story survived and the e stayed out. Other translators have tackled the same task in other languages.

Perec also played the reverse game in **Les Revenentes**, where **e is the only vowel** that appears.

## Why it is good dinner material

It is a rare case where a gimmick turns out to be serious literature. And it gives you a small party game: ask people to describe their day without an e and watch how fast they stumble.
