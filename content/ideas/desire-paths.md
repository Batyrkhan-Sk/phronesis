Look at almost any park, campus or housing estate and you will find one: a bare line worn across the grass where the paved path takes a long way round. That is a **desire path**.

## What it tells you

A desire path is an **unplanned trail** made by many people (or animals) independently choosing the same shortcut. Over time their feet wear away the plants, leaving a track. It usually appears where the formal routes are **longer, have gaps, or don’t exist**.

The idea is a gift to designers. Normally they have to guess how people will move through a space. A desire path is a **free, honest record** of what people actually do, made without surveys or meetings.

## Many names

The same thing goes by many names: **desire line** (the usual term in transport planning), **social trail**, **cow path**, **goat track** and **elephant path**, among others. In transport planning a desire line can also mean a straight line drawn between where people start and where they want to end up, to show demand.

## Using them

Some designers treat desire paths as feedback. The story told in several places is that a campus or site is built without paths, and the paths are laid only after people have shown where they walk. That story is often told without a named place, so treat it as a **good anecdote** rather than a documented method; but the principle, watching behaviour before fixing the layout, is sound.

## A word of caution

Desire paths are not always right. They can damage fragile ground, cross roads unsafely or cut through places that are meant to stay closed. Sometimes the answer is to pave the path; sometimes it is to make the official route better.

## Beyond paths

The idea has spread to **software and everyday design**: people use a product in ways its makers didn’t intend, and the best designers take notice. It is a useful question for any system: *where are people already going, despite the signs?*

## About the name

You will often read that the philosopher **Gaston Bachelard** coined “desire path” in 1958. That attribution is repeated widely but questioned, so it is safest to say the term’s origin is unclear.
