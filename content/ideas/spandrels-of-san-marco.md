Picture a dome resting on a ring of round arches. Where the arches meet, curved triangles of masonry are left over. Medieval and Renaissance builders often decorated them with mosaics of saints or evangelists. The architectural word for such a leftover space is a **spandrel**.

## Borrowing a word

In 1979 the biologists **Stephen Jay Gould** and **Richard Lewontin** used the image of the basilica of San Marco in Venice to make a point about evolution. A visitor who saw only the ornate mosaics might think the triangles existed *in order to* hold the pictures. In fact the spaces are a **by-product** of the architecture, and the decoration came afterwards.

Their warning was about what they called the **adaptationist programme**: the habit of explaining every trait as a finely tuned solution to some problem. Some features, they argued, come along for the ride, and inventing a neat story for each one is storytelling, not evidence. They borrowed the name of Voltaire’s Dr Pangloss, who insisted this was the best of all possible worlds.

## The architects object

Here the story gets a twist worth enjoying. Critics pointed out that the San Marco features are really **pendentives**, a specific kind of curved support, not simple spandrels. The philosopher **Daniel Dennett** went further, arguing that builders chose pendentives among other options and that they do real structural work, so they are not purely accidental.

Gould replied that the word could reasonably be stretched to mean any space left over. Most commentators agree the architectural slip does not damage the biological argument. It is a good reminder that analogies need checking too.

## Why bring it up

The idea travels well. Is a habit, a rule or a tradition there *because* of a clever purpose, or is it a leftover of something else? The next time someone offers a neat explanation, you can ask, kindly, whether it might be a spandrel.

## Spandrels and exaptation

Gould later coined, with Elisabeth Vrba, the term **exaptation** for a trait that now serves a use different from the one it evolved for. A spandrel that gets painted is a tidy picture of this: a by-product that is later put to work.
