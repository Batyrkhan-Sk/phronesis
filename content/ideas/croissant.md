Austrians have baked the crescent-shaped **kipferl** since at least the Middle Ages. It was a richer bread, not the flaky pastry we know.

Around **1838–1839**, an Austrian former artillery officer named **August Zang** opened the **Boulangerie Viennoise** at 92 rue de Richelieu in Paris. His Viennese breads and kipferl were a hit, and Parisian bakers soon copied them. Pastries made with techniques from Vienna are still called **viennoiseries** in French bakeries.

## Where the flakiness came from

Zang’s kipferl was not the croissant of today. The **laminated** dough, folded again and again with butter to create hundreds of thin layers, was developed by **French bakers**. Recipes for something recognisable appear around the turn of the 20th century. So the honest answer to “is the croissant French?” is: *Viennese idea, French perfection*.

## The famous myth

The often-repeated story says Viennese bakers invented the crescent shape in **1683** to celebrate defeating the Ottoman siege, mocking the crescent on Ottoman flags. Food historians, including **Alan Davidson**, have found **no contemporary evidence** for it, and the story appears only much later. Another legend credits Marie Antoinette with bringing it to France. That one is also unsupported.
