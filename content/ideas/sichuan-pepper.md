Eat a dish from **Sichuan** and two sensations arrive together: the burn of **chilli** (*là*) and a strange **tingling numbness** (*má*). Together they make **málà**, the signature of the region’s food. The numbing part comes from **Sichuan pepper**.

## Not a pepper

The husks come from shrubs of the genus ***Zanthoxylum***, in the same family as **citrus** and rue. That’s why they smell lemony. They’re unrelated to black pepper or chillies, and they aren’t actually hot.

## A spice you feel as a vibration

The active molecule, **hydroxy-alpha-sanshool**, excites nerve fibres that normally sense **light touch and vibration**. Your lips feel as though they’re buzzing.

In **2013**, researchers at University College London tested how strong the effect was. Volunteers put Sichuan pepper on their lips and then matched the sensation to a mechanical vibration. The tingle matched a frequency of about **50 Hz**: fifty pulses a second. It works like a vibration your nerves perceive without any movement.

## Why it’s on the menu again in America

From **1968 to 2005** the US banned imports, because the plant could carry **citrus canker**, a disease of citrus trees. The ban was loosely enforced, but Sichuan food in American restaurants was often made without it, so many people only discovered the real *má* in the last couple of decades.

Before chillies reached China in the 16th century, Sichuan pepper was one of the main sources of pungency in Chinese cooking.
