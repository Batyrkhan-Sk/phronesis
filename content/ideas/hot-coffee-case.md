If you know one thing about this case, it is probably the joke: someone spilled coffee on herself and won millions. The reality is more sobering, and a good example of how a story can drift from its facts.

## What happened

In **1992**, **Stella Liebeck**, then **79**, bought a coffee at a McDonald’s drive-through in **Albuquerque, New Mexico**. As a passenger in her nephew’s car, she held the cup between her knees to add cream and sugar, and it spilled. She suffered **third-degree burns**, spent **eight days in hospital** and needed skin grafts. Sources differ on how much of her body was affected, so it is safest to leave the percentage out.

## Before the trial

Liebeck did not start by suing for millions. She asked McDonald’s to pay about **$20,000** towards her medical and related costs. The company offered **$800**. Only then did she bring a case.

At trial her lawyers showed that McDonald’s required its coffee to be held at roughly **180–190°F (82–88°C)**, hotter than many competitors served, and that the company had received **more than 700 earlier complaints** of burns. A hotter drink burns skin faster, which is why the temperature was central.

## The numbers

The jury awarded **$200,000** in compensatory damages, cut to **$160,000** because it judged Liebeck **20 per cent** at fault. It also awarded **$2.7 million** in punitive damages, meant to punish the company’s conduct. The judge reduced this to **$480,000**, three times the compensatory figure. The parties then settled privately before an appeal was decided; the amount was never made public.

## What to take from it

The headline numbers circulated without the context. Whatever you think of the outcome, it is worth knowing it began with a seriously injured person and a request for a modest sum. Being the person at dinner who adds that detail kindly, without correcting anyone harshly, is a small art of its own.

## Why the joke stuck

The case arrived in the middle of a political argument over lawsuits and damages, and a short, funny version travelled further than the long, accurate one. Accounts today still differ on details such as the settlement figure, so the honest summary is the court record,, not either side’s retelling.
