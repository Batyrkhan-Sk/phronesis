**Ludwik Lejzer Zamenhof** grew up in Białystok, then part of the Russian Empire, where Poles, Russians, Germans and Jews lived side by side, often in mutual suspicion. He blamed part of the hostility on **language barriers**. His solution was a neutral, easy second language that belonged to no nation.

On **26 July 1887** he published it under the pen name **Doktoro Esperanto**: “one who hopes”. The name stuck to the language.

## Designed to be easy

- Spelling is fully phonetic.
- Grammar has no irregular verbs.
- Word endings show the part of speech: nouns end in **-o**, adjectives in **-a**.
- Vocabulary is mostly drawn from Romance and Germanic languages.

## Native speakers

Esperanto speakers meet at international congresses. Some marry, and their children grow up hearing Esperanto at home. These native speakers, called **denaskuloj**, number around **a thousand**. That makes Esperanto a rare case: an invented language with a living, native community.

## Dangerous internationalism

Totalitarian regimes saw it as a threat. **Hitler** attacked it in *Mein Kampf* (Zamenhof was Jewish), and it was **banned in Germany in 1935**. Under **Stalin**, from 1937, many Soviet Esperantists were imprisoned or executed as suspected spies, because of their foreign correspondence.

Estimates of today’s speakers vary enormously, from **tens of thousands to two million**, because nobody agrees on what counts as “speaking” it.
