In 1978, psychologists interviewed **22 major lottery winners** and **29 people paralysed in accidents**, plus a control group. The winners were **not significantly happier** than the controls, and found less pleasure in everyday things like a compliment or breakfast. The accident victims rated themselves less happy, but **less unhappy than most people would predict**.

The sample was tiny and the details are often overstated. The core idea has held up well though: **we adapt**.

## How it works

A pay rise, a new car or a bigger house feels wonderful, and then it becomes **normal**. Expectations rise to meet circumstances. This is the *hedonic treadmill*: you keep moving and your satisfaction stays roughly where it was.

## The important nuances

Research since the 2000s shows the baseline is **not fixed**:
- Some events, such as **long-term unemployment** or severe disability, can lower wellbeing for years.
- Different parts of wellbeing (moment-to-moment mood versus life satisfaction) adapt differently.
- People’s baselines differ, and can change.

## A useful takeaway

Adaptation is fastest for **things** and slowest for **experiences, relationships and variety**. Spending on shared experiences, small and varied pleasures, and time rather than objects tends to resist the treadmill best.
