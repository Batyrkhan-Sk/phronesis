Hebrew never died in the usual sense. For nearly two millennia it was read, prayed, studied and written: legal commentaries, poetry and letters between scholars. But from roughly the **2nd century CE**, it was nobody’s **first language**. No child grew up with Hebrew as the language of the kitchen and the playground.

## A family experiment

**Eliezer Ben-Yehuda** (1858–1922) moved to Jerusalem in 1881 convinced that a Jewish national revival needed a shared spoken language. He insisted that his household speak **only Hebrew**. His son **Itamar Ben-Avi**, born in **1882**, is described as the first native speaker of Modern Hebrew.

Ben-Yehuda also started a vast **dictionary**, coining words for things the ancient language never needed, from newspapers to ice cream.

## Not one man’s achievement

The popular story credits Ben-Yehuda alone, but historians point to the **schools and agricultural settlements** of the First and Second Aliyah (waves of immigration from the 1880s onwards). There, teachers taught in Hebrew and children from many countries, speaking Yiddish, Russian, Arabic and Ladino, adopted it as their **common language**. Ben-Yehuda was the symbol. The children did the reviving.

## Why it worked when others haven’t

Linguists often say revival is close to impossible: once intergenerational transmission stops, a language rarely returns. Hebrew had unusual advantages:

- a huge written literature, so vocabulary and grammar were never lost;
- a population of immigrants with **no shared language**, so they needed one;
- schools, and later a state, that used it for everything.

Revival projects for Cornish, Manx and Hawaiian draw lessons from it. None has had all three advantages at once.
