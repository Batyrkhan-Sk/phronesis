Ask almost anyone what Sherlock Holmes says when he explains a deduction and you will hear the same line: **“Elementary, my dear Watson.”** It is on mugs, in parodies and in countless films. It is not in the books.

## What Conan Doyle actually wrote

Across Arthur Conan Doyle’s four novels and 56 short stories, the full phrase never appears. The two halves do, separately:

- In **“The Crooked Man”** (1893), Watson reports Holmes replying to him with the single word, “Elementary”.
- Holmes says **“my dear Watson”** in several stories, and often in the form “Exactly, my dear Watson”, which may be why the memory feels right.

So the line is a remix of real fragments, not an invention from nothing.

## Who put it together?

The most-cited early example is in **P. G. Wodehouse’s** comic novel *Psmith, Journalist* (1915), where the character Psmith, playing at detective, says “Elementary, my dear Watson, elementary.” The Oxford Dictionary of Quotations points to that book. Another reference work credits the 1929 film *The Return of Sherlock Holmes*.

Neither is the whole story. A Yale researcher of quotations has reported finding the phrase in American newspapers from 1909 and 1911, and a 1899 stage play had a close cousin, “Oh, this is elementary, my dear fellow”. So “Wodehouse coined it” is a handy shorthand, but the true origin is **disputed** and may be a piece of playful popular speech that was already in the air.

## Why it stuck

By the 1920s and 1930s, films and radio needed Holmes to sound unmistakably like Holmes. A short, confident, slightly teasing catchphrase did that better than anything in the original prose. Audiences then carried it back to the books in their heads.

It is a lovely example of how a character can be completed by his audience. If you want to share it kindly, you don’t need to correct anyone: you can just say that the real Holmes comes close, and let people enjoy the discovery.
