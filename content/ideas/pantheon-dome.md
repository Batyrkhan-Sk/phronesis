Walk into the **Pantheon** in Rome and look up. A great round dome, with a circle of open sky at the top, hangs over you. There is no steel hidden in it. It was finished almost two thousand years ago, and it is still the **largest unreinforced concrete dome** in the world.

## What you are looking at

The dome is about **43 metres across**. The inside is a cunning shape: the height from the floor to the top equals the width, so a perfect sphere would fit inside the building and just touch the floor.

The inscription on the front reads that Marcus Agrippa built it. That is a reference to an *earlier* temple that Agrippa founded on the site. The present building dates from the time of the emperor **Hadrian**, in the 120s AD; sources differ by a few years on the exact dates.

## How it stays up

Concrete is strong when squeezed and weak when pulled. The Romans handled this in several ways at once:

- **Lighter at the top.** The aggregate, the stones mixed into the concrete, changes as the dome rises, from heavier material low down to lighter material such as pumice and volcanic tuff near the crown.
- **Thinner at the top.** The shell is thickest at the base and thins towards the opening.
- **Coffers.** The sunken square panels on the inside remove weight without weakening the shape. They are also pleasing to look at.
- **The hole.** The round opening at the top, the **oculus**, lets light in and leaves no heavy central stone to support.

## A building that was never abandoned

Many Roman buildings were stripped for stone. The Pantheon was handed to the Church in **609**, and has been used ever since. It is reasonable to think that continuous use is a large part of why it survives, though that is an interpretation rather than something anyone can prove.

## One line for the table

If someone says “they don’t make them like they used to”, the Pantheon is the rare case where it is true, and the dome is the reason.
