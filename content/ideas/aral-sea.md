In **1960** the Aral Sea, on the border of today’s **Kazakhstan** and **Uzbekistan**, covered about **68,000 km²**: the **fourth-largest lake** in the world, with busy fishing ports and its own fleet.

## Draining a sea

Two great rivers fed it: the **Amu Darya** and the **Syr Darya**. From the early 1960s, Soviet planners diverted them into vast irrigation canals to grow **cotton**, rice and melons in the desert. The rivers stopped reaching the sea.

The Aral shrank year after year. It split into a northern and southern part in **1987**, and salinity rose until most native fish died. Ships were left rusting on dry land, kilometres from the water. By **2014** satellite images showed the eastern basin of the South Aral had **dried up completely**, leaving the **Aralkum**, a new desert of salty dust.

## The comeback

Kazakhstan, which controls the northern part, chose a practical fix. With support from the **World Bank**, it built the **Kokaral dam**, completed in **August 2005**, to keep the Syr Darya’s water in the **North Aral** instead of letting it drain into the dying south.

The results surprised many people:
- the water level rose from below **30 m** to about **42 m** by 2008;
- salinity fell from about **30 to 8 grams per litre**;
- freshwater fish returned, and **commercial fishing** came back to towns like Aralsk.

## The lesson

The South Aral, mostly in Uzbekistan, is still largely lost, and full restoration of the old sea isn’t realistic. But the North Aral shows that some environmental damage can be **partly reversed** with focused engineering and the will to do it.
