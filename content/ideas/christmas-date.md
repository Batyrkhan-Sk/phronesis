The Gospels describe Jesus’s birth, but **no date**. For the first few centuries, Christians mostly celebrated **Easter**; birthdays weren’t a major focus.

## The first record

The earliest evidence for **25 December** is the **Chronograph of 354**, a Roman calendar that reflects church practice around **336**. By the end of the 4th century the date had spread through much of the Christian world.

## Two explanations

**1. The solstice and the sun.** In the Roman calendar, 25 December was the **winter solstice**, and from **274** the emperor Aurelian promoted a festival of ***Sol Invictus***, the “Unconquered Sun”, on that day. On this view, the church placed Christ’s birth on a date already associated with light returning. Saturnalia, a December festival of feasting and gifts, adds to the picture.

**2. The calculation.** Some early Christians believed Jesus was **conceived** on the same date he would die, which they placed on **25 March**, the Roman date of the spring equinox. Add **nine months** and you get **25 December**. This explanation doesn’t need a pagan festival at all.

Historians still debate which mattered more. Both may have played a part.

## Christmas on 7 January

Many **Eastern Orthodox** churches, such as those of Russia, Serbia and Georgia, still follow the older **Julian calendar** for church dates. Their 25 December currently falls on the Gregorian **7 January**. They celebrate the same day, counted on a different calendar.
