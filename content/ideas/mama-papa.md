Mandarin *māma*, Swahili *mama*, Persian *mâmân*, Spanish *mamá*, Polish *mama*. These languages are not closely related, and some are not related at all. So why do they agree?

## The babbling explanation

Babies start with the easiest sounds: an open mouth (**a**) and lips coming together (**m**, **p**, **b**). Around six months, babbling produces “**ma-ma-ma**”, and soon after “**pa-pa**”, “**ba-ba**” and “**da-da**”.

The babies are probably just playing with sound. But **parents hear their names**. Since mothers have usually been the main early caregivers, the earliest syllable, typically *ma*, gets attached to them, and the next ones to the father. The linguist **Larry Trask** made this argument; parents, he suggested, are keen to hear what they want to hear.

The linguist **Roman Jakobson** added a physical detail: the soft nasal hum babies make while feeding, with lips pressed together, sounds like *mmm*.

## The exceptions prove the rule

- **Georgian:** *mama* means **father**, and *deda* means **mother**.
- **Turkish:** *ana* for mother, *baba* for father.
- **Old Japanese** used *papa* for **mother**.

If the words were inherited, these reversals would be hard to explain. If they come from babbling that adults then interpret, they are exactly what you’d expect: the sounds are universal, but which parent gets which sound is up to each culture.
