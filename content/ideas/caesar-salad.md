During **Prohibition** (1920–1933), Americans crossed the border to drink legally. **Tijuana** boomed with bars, casinos and restaurants. **Caesar Cardini**, an Italian immigrant who had run restaurants in San Diego, opened one there.

According to his daughter Rosa, on **4 July 1924** the restaurant was so busy that the kitchen ran low. Cardini assembled what he had, romaine lettuce, croutons, Parmesan, egg, lemon, olive oil and **Worcestershire sauce**, and made a show of tossing it **at the table**. The family says the original had no anchovies; their faint flavour came from the Worcestershire sauce.

## Julia Child’s memory

The chef **Julia Child** wrote that her parents took her to Caesar’s restaurant as a child in the 1920s and that she remembered the salad being prepared at the table. It is one of the best pieces of early evidence for the story.

## A naming lesson

The salad is named after **Caesar Cardini, not Julius Caesar**. A Mexican-invented, Italian-American, Prohibition-era dish with a Roman-sounding name is a good example of how tangled food history usually is.
