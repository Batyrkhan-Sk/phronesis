In 1968 the crew of Apollo 8 became the first people to see the whole Earth rise over the Moon’s horizon. Many of the astronauts who followed have tried to describe what looking at our planet from space felt like, and their descriptions overlap more than you might expect.

## The name

The author **Frank White** interviewed astronauts in the 1980s and gave this recurring theme a name, the **overview effect**, in his 1987 book. Typical themes in the accounts are:

- how **thin** the atmosphere looks, a delicate film around the planet;
- the absence of **borders**, which exist on maps and in law but not to the eye;
- a sense that everyone shares **one small home**.

White quotes the Apollo 14 astronaut **Edgar Mitchell** describing an “explosion of awareness” on the journey back from the Moon.

## What kind of thing is it?

Psychologists have treated it as a strong case of **awe**, the emotion we feel in front of something vast that challenges how we usually see the world. A 2016 paper by David Yaden and colleagues examined astronaut accounts and linked the experience to **self-transcendence**: a feeling that the self matters less and the whole matters more.

## What we should be careful about

The honest version is modest. The evidence is mostly **personal testimony**, often given years later and by people who chose to become astronauts. Not everyone reports the same thing, and “it changed my values for good” is a claim about long-term change that is hard to test. It is still a striking pattern, and a useful one to bring up, because it suggests that **distance changes what we notice**.

You don’t need a rocket to borrow a little of it. The idea is the same one behind the [Pale Blue Dot](idea:pale-blue-dot): step far enough back and the arguments that fill our days shrink next to the single fact that this is where all of us live.
