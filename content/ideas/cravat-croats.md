If you are wearing a tie, you are wearing a piece of **Croatian military history**, whether you meant to or not.

## A soldier’s scarf

During the **Thirty Years’ War** (1618–1648), mercenaries from the Croatian Military Frontier fought across Europe, and some served in France. They wore small knotted neckcloths, and Parisians noticed. The French word **cravate** came to describe this neckwear, and the word itself is simply a form of the French name for a Croat. Military historian Filip Hren has described the war as the point when the cravat first gained wide recognition in Europe.

## A regiment and a boy king

France went on to recruit Croats into cavalry units that became known as the **Royal Cravates**. Sources differ on the details, so treat the date of the 1660s as approximate.

The style then crossed from the barracks to the court. **Louis XIV** is credited with popularising it (not inventing it): he is said to have worn a lace cravat from about 1646, when he was a child, and the French nobility followed. English picked up the word quickly: the *Oxford English Dictionary* records it from **1656**.

## From cravat to tie

The cravat was a cloth tied or knotted at the throat, often in lace or linen. The long, narrow **necktie** we know is a nineteenth-century development, a descendant rather than the same object. That is also why many languages still use the old word: *cravate* in French, *Krawatte* in German, *kravata* in Croatian.

## Why it is nice to know

It is a rare case where a nation’s name ended up around the world’s necks. If you are talking to someone from Croatia, it is a warm and accurate thing to mention.
