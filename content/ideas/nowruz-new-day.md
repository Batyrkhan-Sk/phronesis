**Nowruz** is Persian for “new day”. It marks the first day of spring and is celebrated by communities from Iran and Afghanistan to Central Asia, the Caucasus and Turkey, and by diaspora communities worldwide. In **2009** UNESCO inscribed it on its list of intangible cultural heritage, and in **2010** the UN General Assembly named **21 March** the International Day of Nowruz. The resolution was co-sponsored by countries including Iran, Afghanistan, Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkey and Turkmenistan.

## A year that starts with astronomy

Nowruz is tied to the **vernal equinox**, when day and night are close to equal length. In the Iranian calendar the year begins at the exact moment of the equinox, which is why the precise time differs with the time zone, and the date can fall on 20 or 21 March. Sources credit the eleventh-century reform of the Iranian calendar, associated with **Omar Khayyam**, with fixing the new year to the equinox.

## The Haft-sin table

In Iran the family sets a table called the **Haft-sin**, with seven items whose names begin with the Persian letter *S*, such as *sabzeh* (sprouted grain), *sib* (apple) and *sir* (garlic). Each carries a meaning of renewal, health or prosperity, though families explain the symbols a little differently.

## Nauryz in Kazakhstan

In Kazakhstan the festival is **Nauryz**, a public holiday around **22 March**, recognised by decree in 1991. Families gather around a large table, yurts are set up in public squares, and the signature dish is **Nauryz kozhe**, usually described as made from seven ingredients such as meat, grains and milk. Sources differ on the exact list and on how many days the celebration runs, so check the current official announcement.

## How old is it?

Popular articles say “3,000 years”. Scholars are more careful. Some historians link Nowruz to Zoroastrian and Achaemenid traditions, and Persepolis is often connected to New Year ceremonies, but the term itself is not clearly attested in early inscriptions and the earliest Zoroastrian sources say little about it. “Ancient, widely shared and very old in its roots” is safer than any precise number.

If you want to be generous at a dinner table, ask a friend how their family marks it. The answers are rarely the same, and that is part of the point.
