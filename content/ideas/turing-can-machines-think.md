## The machine that can be any machine

In **1936**, aged 24, **Alan Turing** published a paper on a problem in mathematical logic. To solve it, he imagined a very simple device that reads and writes symbols on a tape, following a table of rules. He showed that one such machine, a **universal machine**, could imitate any other, given the right instructions on its tape.

That idea, a single machine that does any computation given the right program, is the theoretical heart of every computer. It also raised an obvious question: if reasoning can be written as rules, can a machine reason?

## “Can machines think?”

During the war Turing worked as a codebreaker at Bletchley Park. In **October 1950** he published “**Computing Machinery and Intelligence**” in the philosophy journal *Mind*. It opens: “I propose to consider the question, ‘**Can machines think?**’”

Since “think” is hard to define, he replaced the question with a game. A judge exchanges typed messages with a hidden person and a hidden machine. If the judge can’t reliably tell which is which, Turing argued, there is no good reason to deny that the machine thinks. This is the **imitation game**, now called the **Turing test**.

## Answering Ada Lovelace

Turing went through objections one by one. One he called **Lady Lovelace’s Objection**: in 1843 Ada Lovelace had written that the Analytical Engine “has no pretensions whatever to **originate** anything”; it can only do what we order it to. Turing replied that machines **surprise** us all the time, because we can’t foresee all the consequences of their instructions.

## The child machine

Turing’s most prophetic idea was near the end. Instead of programming an adult mind directly, he suggested, build a **“child machine”** and **educate** it, rewarding and correcting it, so that it learns. He compared the process to **evolution**.

That is, in outline, how modern machine learning works: systems aren’t told the rules, they learn them from vast amounts of examples and feedback. Whether passing his test would mean a machine really *understands* is still argued over, and Turing himself thought the original question was “too meaningless to deserve discussion”.
