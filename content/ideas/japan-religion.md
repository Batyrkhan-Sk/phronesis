Ask whether someone in Japan is Shinto or Buddhist and the honest answer is often “both”, or “neither”.

## More believers than people

Japan’s **Agency for Cultural Affairs** collects membership figures from religious organisations. In 2015 they counted roughly **70% Shinto** and **70% Buddhist** adherents, together far more than the population. Shrines and temples each count the same families.

Yet when people are surveyed about personal belief, a large majority, **72%** in a 2013 national survey, say they have **no religion**. For many people, religion in Japan is about **practice and custom**, not about declaring a belief.

## Life in three traditions

There’s a well-known saying: Japanese people are **born Shinto, marry Christian and die Buddhist**.

- **Birth and childhood:** babies are presented at **Shinto shrines**, and families visit shrines at New Year and for festivals.
- **Weddings:** **Christian-style** ceremonies, with a white dress and a chapel, made up roughly **60–70%** of weddings around 2015, though Christians are only about **1–2%** of the population.
- **Death:** around **91%** of funerals are **Buddhist**, and family graves are kept at Buddhist temples.

## A long history of mixing

For over a thousand years Shinto and Buddhism were deeply combined: shrines had temples and gods were seen as forms of buddhas. In **1868** the Meiji government ordered them separated and promoted State Shinto. In everyday life, though, the mixing never fully stopped.
