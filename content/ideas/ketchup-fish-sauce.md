In the coastal regions of southern China, fishermen made a pungent sauce from salted, fermented fish. In the Hokkien language it was called something like **kê-tsiap**: the brine of pickled fish or shellfish.

British traders in Southeast Asia met sauces like it in the 17th and 18th centuries and took the idea, and probably the name, home.

## The British ketchups

Back in Britain, “ketchup” came to mean almost any **dark, salty, spiced sauce** used to season food. Recipes from the period use:

- mushrooms (mushroom ketchup is still sold in Britain)
- oysters and mussels
- walnuts
- grapes and egg whites

None of them were sweet, and none were red.

## Enter the tomato

In **1812**, the American scientist and horticulturist **James Mease** published the first known **tomato** ketchup recipe. Over the 19th century, American manufacturers added more sugar and vinegar, and tomato ketchup became the default.

The linguist **Dan Jurafsky** uses ketchup as his favourite example of how food words travel: the name survived a journey across half the world while the sauce changed almost completely.
