**Joseph Weizenbaum**, a computer scientist at **MIT**, wrote **ELIZA** between **1964 and 1967**. Its best-known script, **DOCTOR**, played a **Rogerian psychotherapist**, a style of therapy in which the therapist mostly reflects the patient’s words back.

That made it easy to fake:

> **User:** I’m unhappy at work.
> **ELIZA:** Why do you say you are unhappy at work?
> **User:** My boss doesn’t listen to me.
> **ELIZA:** Tell me more about your boss.

ELIZA had no understanding at all. It matched keywords and rearranged sentences using templates.

## The secretary

People responded as if it understood them anyway. Weizenbaum’s own **secretary**, who knew how it worked, asked him to **leave the room** so she could talk to it privately. Some psychiatrists suggested automated therapy might soon be practical.

## Its creator’s alarm

Weizenbaum was disturbed. He wrote that “extremely short exposures to a relatively simple computer program could induce **powerful delusional thinking** in quite normal people.” In ***Computer Power and Human Reason*** (1976) he argued that human thought can’t be reduced to computation, and that some decisions, involving judgement, care and ethics, should **never** be handed to machines, whether or not they could make them.

## The ELIZA effect

Today, the **ELIZA effect** means our tendency to attribute understanding and feelings to programs that produce human-like text. The modern chatbots are vastly more capable than ELIZA, which makes Weizenbaum’s questions more relevant, not less.
