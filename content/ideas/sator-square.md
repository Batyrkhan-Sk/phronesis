```
S A T O R
A R E P O
T E N E T
O P E R A
R O T A S
```

Read it **left to right, right to left, top to bottom or bottom to top**: you get the same five words every time. The central word **TENET** forms a cross.

Roughly, the Latin means *“The sower Arepo holds the wheels with effort”*. **AREPO** is the problem: it appears nowhere else in Latin and may be an invented name made to fit the puzzle.

## Where it turns up

Examples were scratched into walls in **Pompeii**, and so must date from before the eruption of 79 AD. Others were found at Dura-Europos in Syria, and in later centuries the square appears on church walls, in manuscripts, on amulets, and in folk charms against fire and fever. It was still being written on things in the 1800s.

## In cinema

Christopher Nolan’s *Tenet* (2020) uses all five words: the villain **Sator**, the forger **Arepo**, the organisation **Tenet**, the **opera** house siege at the start, and **Rotas** Security. The film is about time running backwards, which makes it a fitting borrowing for a puzzle that reads the same in both directions.

## The Paternoster theory

In 1926 Felix Grosser noticed something remarkable. The 25 letters can be rearranged into **PATERNOSTER** (“Our Father”) written twice in a cross sharing the central N, with two **A**s and two **O**s left over. These could stand for **Alpha and Omega**, a Christian symbol for God.

```
       A
       P
       A
       T
       E
       R
A PATERNOSTER O
       O
       S
       T
       E
       R
       O
```

It looks like proof of a hidden Christian code. But the Pompeii finds complicate it: a fully developed Christian cryptogram in a small Italian town before 79 AD would be surprisingly early, and the Alpha-and-Omega symbolism is associated with the later Book of Revelation. Some scholars think it could be **Jewish**, others think the PATERNOSTER anagram is a **coincidence** (25 letters allow a lot of rearrangements), and others think it began as **pure wordplay** and was given Christian meaning later.

This is a good example of distinguishing **established facts** (the find sites and the anagram itself) from **interpretations** (what the makers intended).
