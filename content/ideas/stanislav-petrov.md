September **1983** was one of the most tense moments of the Cold War. Three weeks earlier the Soviet Union had shot down a Korean airliner, and Soviet leaders feared a surprise American attack.

Shortly after midnight on **26 September**, at the Serpukhov-15 early-warning bunker near Moscow, the new **Oko** satellite system raised an alarm: **one** US intercontinental ballistic missile had been launched. Then **four more**.

## The decision

The officer on duty, **Lieutenant Colonel Stanislav Petrov**, had to decide whether to pass the warning up the chain of command as a real attack. He reasoned:

- A genuine American first strike would launch **hundreds** of missiles at once, not five.
- The satellite system was **new** and not yet fully trusted.
- **Ground radars** showed nothing.

He reported a **system malfunction**. Minutes passed, and no missiles arrived.

## The cause

Investigators found that a rare alignment of **sunlight on high-altitude clouds** with the satellites’ orbits had looked like rocket launches.

## No medal

Petrov was first praised, then **reprimanded** for incomplete paperwork. Rewarding him would have meant admitting the system had failed. The story became public only in **1998**. He died in 2017, aged 77.

Historians debate how close the world really came: there were other people and other checks in the chain of command. But the 1983 incident is often cited as one of the closest calls of the nuclear age, and as a lesson about keeping **human judgement** between automated warnings and irreversible actions.
