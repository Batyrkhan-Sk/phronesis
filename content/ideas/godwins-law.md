If you’ve ever watched an online argument run on for long enough, you may have seen someone eventually compare the other side to the Nazis. **Godwin’s law** is the name for that tendency.

## The wording

The most-quoted version is: **as an online discussion grows longer, the probability of a comparison involving Nazis or Hitler approaches 1.** It sounds like mathematics, and that was part of the joke.

## An experiment, not just a joke

**Mike Godwin**, an American lawyer, came up with it on Usenet in 1990. What many people don’t know is that it was **deliberate**. Godwin has described it as an experiment in **memetic engineering**: he thought comparisons to the Nazis were being thrown around so freely that they had lost their meaning, and he set out to create a **counter-meme** that would make people stop and think before using them.

By that measure it worked rather well: the term is now in the Oxford English Dictionary and is used across the internet.

## What it doesn’t say

Two misreadings are common.

- **“Whoever mentions Hitler first loses.”** That rule grew up on Usenet. It isn’t in Godwin’s own wording, and he has said it’s an oversimplification.
- **“You may never make the comparison.”** Godwin has said that some comparisons are fair, and the law is meant to encourage thought, not to ban history. Using it to wave away any comparison at all misses its purpose.

## Why it makes good conversation

It’s a rare internet adage with a known author and a clear intent, and the intent is generous: it asks people to respect the weight of the worst events in history. If someone cites it to end an argument, you can gently point out that the person who wrote it wanted the opposite: more care, not less thought.
