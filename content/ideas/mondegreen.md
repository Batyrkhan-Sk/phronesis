Almost everyone has sung a lyric wrong and been told so, kindly or otherwise. English has a word for it, and the word has a lovely backstory.

## The woman who wasn’t there

As a child, the American writer **Sylvia Wright** was read a Scottish ballad, *The Bonnie Earl o’ Moray*, by her mother. It tells of the killing of the Earl of Moray, and one line says the murderers *“laid him on the green”*. Wright heard it as *“Lady Mondegreen”*: a tender companion who died beside the earl.

She later admitted she preferred her version. The real line gave the earl nobody to die with. In a 1954 essay in *Harper’s Magazine* she proposed a name for the whole phenomenon: the **mondegreen**.

## What counts as one

A mondegreen is a mishearing that **makes a new sense** of the sounds. It is not the same as simply forgetting words, and it is not the same as a deliberate pun. The mishearer usually ends up with something that feels meaningful, which is why it sticks. A frequently cited example is hearing “kiss the sky” in Jimi Hendrix’s *Purple Haze* as “kiss this guy”.

The pattern points to how listening works. We do not decode sound one syllable at a time. We **predict** what is likely and fit the noise to it, so when a lyric is unclear, the brain supplies something plausible. That is an interpretation of the evidence rather than a settled theory of any one case, but it explains why mondegreens are so common and so confident.

## A word that grew up

Wright’s coinage spread slowly, then became standard. It appeared in the Random House Webster’s College Dictionary in 2000, the Oxford English Dictionary in 2002 and Merriam-Webster’s Collegiate Dictionary in 2008.

## Using it kindly

The pleasure of the word is that it lets you turn an embarrassing moment into a good story. Ask a friend for their favourite misheard lyric and you will usually get a laugh and a childhood memory. Be generous: nearly everybody has one.
