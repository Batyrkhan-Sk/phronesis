In the 1950s, the Soviet Union needed a launch site for its new intercontinental missiles: flat, remote, and far from prying eyes. They chose the steppe near the railway station of **Tyuratam** in southern **Kazakhstan**.

## Firsts

From there:
- **4 October 1957**: **Sputnik 1**, the first artificial satellite, went into orbit;
- **12 April 1961**: **Yuri Gagarin** became the first human in space, in Vostok 1.

Later came the first woman in space, Valentina Tereshkova (1963), and many crews to the Mir and International space stations. For years after the Space Shuttle was retired, the only way for astronauts to reach the ISS was a Russian Soyuz from Baikonur.

## A rented cosmodrome

When the USSR broke up in 1991, Baikonur ended up in independent Kazakhstan. Russia now **leases** the cosmodrome and the town that serves it. The current agreement runs until **2050**, at about **US$115 million a year**. The town of Baikonur is administered under Russian arrangements, an unusual case of a city in one country run by another.

## A misleading name?

There *is* a mining town called **Baikonur**, about 320 km from the launch site. A popular story says the Soviets borrowed its name **deliberately**, to send Western intelligence looking in the wrong place. NASA’s history office disagrees, saying Baikonur was already the name of the wider region. It’s a good story, but treat it as disputed.

The world’s space age began on the Kazakh steppe, a fact many people don’t know.
