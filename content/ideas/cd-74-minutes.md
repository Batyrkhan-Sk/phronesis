If you ask why a compact disc plays for about **74 minutes**, you will probably hear: *because Beethoven’s Ninth Symphony had to fit.* It is one of the best-loved stories in consumer technology. It is also a story worth telling carefully.

## What is well documented

In **1979** Sony and Philips formed a joint task force to design a digital audio disc, and in **1980** they published the **Red Book** standard. Philips’s early prototype used a smaller **11.5 cm** disc with about **60 minutes** of music.

**Norio Ohga**, a senior Sony figure with a classical music background, wanted more. The disc grew to **12 cm** and the playing time to **74 minutes**. The size was still small enough to be portable. Ohga is reported to have said a longer disc could hold a whole opera, or all of Beethoven’s Ninth.

## Where the story gets slippery

The popular version says the length was **fixed by** the Ninth. The record is thinner than that. Accounts differ on whether Ohga named the symphony as a requirement or only as an example, and fact-checkers rate the strongest form of the claim as unproven.

A second thread names a **1951 Bayreuth recording** conducted by **Wilhelm Furtwängler**, said to run about 74 minutes, and often claimed to have set the target. That is a plausible tale, but we cannot show that the engineers worked from it. Other writers point to more mundane engineering and manufacturing reasons for the final figures.

## How to tell it

A good way to share this is as a two-part story: the **documented** part (a bigger disc, pushed by an executive who loved classical music) and the **charming** part (Beethoven). Say which is which, and you are being honest and good company at once.

And a bonus for your next dinner: the first **commercial** CDs and players went on sale in **Japan on 1 October 1982**, and the album usually named as first, Billy Joel’s *52nd Street*, was simply one of about fifty titles released that day.
