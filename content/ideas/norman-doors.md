You walk up to a glass door, pull the handle, and nothing happens. A sign says **push**. You feel foolish. Designers would say you shouldn’t.

## The idea

**Don Norman**, in *The Design of Everyday Things* (first published as *The Psychology of Everyday Things*), argued that when people keep making the same mistake with an object, the object is badly designed. Doors that give the wrong signal are now called **Norman doors**, and the usual proof is that they need a label to explain themselves.

## Two words worth owning

- An **affordance** is what an object lets you do: a door can be pushed or pulled. Norman borrowed the term from the psychologist **James J. Gibson**.
- A **signifier** is the cue that tells you which action to take. Norman introduced the word in the 2013 revised edition because people kept using “affordance” for both.

## Reading a door

Hardware speaks. A **flat plate** says “push me”. A **long, graspable bar or handle** says “pull me”. A Norman door breaks that grammar, for example by putting a pull handle on a door that must be pushed.

Adding a sign is the common fix, but it often fails: people look at the shape of the handle before they read, so a “push” label on a pull handle can still get pulled. That is an observation from designers rather than a law.

## Why it’s a good thing to know

Next time someone struggles with a door, you can say it isn’t their fault, and you will be right. The same lens works on shower taps, stove knobs and websites.

## Beyond doors

The Norman-door idea is a small version of a bigger claim: good design makes the right action obvious and the wrong action hard. When it fails, we tend to blame ourselves, which hides the design flaw and lets it persist.
