The standard joke about **4′33″** is that it is a famous piece of music with no music in it. The joke is half right. A performer does sit at a piano and does not play. But the composer thought of it as the reverse of silence.

## What happened in Woodstock

On **29 August 1952**, the pianist **David Tudor** gave the premiere at the Maverick Concert Hall near Woodstock, New York, as part of a recital of contemporary piano music. He sat down, closed the keyboard lid, and later opened and closed it again to mark the **three movements**. Nobody played a note. What the audience heard instead was the hall: wind in the trees, rain on the roof, and people shifting in their seats.

## Why Cage did it

Two experiences in 1951 are usually named. One was the **white paintings** of his friend **Robert Rauschenberg**, plain white canvases that look different as the light and shadows in the room change. Cage later wrote that the white paintings came first and his silent piece came later.

The other was a visit to an **anechoic chamber** at Harvard, a room built to absorb nearly all sound. Cage expected silence and heard two sounds, one high and one low. The engineer told him they were his nervous system and his blood circulating. His conclusion was that there is no such thing as a truly silent room while you are alive in it.

So the piece is a frame. Cage set aside four minutes and thirty-three seconds and asked people to treat whatever happened as the music.

## How to say it well

The accurate version is not “Cage wrote silence”. It is that he wrote a piece that **cannot be silent**, and that is why the length is fixed but the sound is different at every performance. How much the chamber and the paintings each shaped the idea is an interpretation, since Cage told the story in several ways over the years.

## Is it music?

People still argue about this, and it is a fair argument. Supporters say that the definition of music is exactly what the piece puts under pressure, and that a concert hall is a rare place where we agree to listen carefully. Critics say that a frame is not a composition. You do not need to settle it to enjoy bringing it up: the best question to ask a friend is simply what they would hear if they sat quietly for four minutes and thirty-three seconds, right now.
