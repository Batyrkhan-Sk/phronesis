There are several theories about the word **Kazakh** (*qazaq*), and none is certain. The most widely repeated one traces it to a Turkic root meaning a **wanderer**: someone **free and independent**, who has broken away to live on their own terms. Other theories connect it to a word for a cart used to carry a yurt, or to an old verb meaning “to gain”.

## The Cossack connection

The **Cossacks** of Ukraine and southern Russia were bands of free frontier horsemen. Their name comes from the **same Kipchak word**. For a long time Russian used one form for both peoples. Only from the **17th century** did writers spell them differently, **Казах** (Kazakh) and **Казак** (Cossack), and the Soviet Union made that official in **1936**.

So two peoples with very different histories carry, in effect, the same name: *the free ones*.

## Breaking away

The story the name tells fits the origin of the Kazakh state. In the **15th century**, two Chingisid princes, **Kerei and Janibek**, led their followers away from the Uzbek khan Abulkhair and founded the **Kazakh Khanate**, traditionally dated to **1465**. Those who left were remembered as the ones who chose freedom over staying.

Later the Kazakhs organised into three **zhuz** (often translated as “hordes”): the Senior, Middle and Junior, roughly corresponding to the south-east, centre-north and west of today’s Kazakhstan. Many Kazakhs still know which zhuz they belong to.

A name about leaving and being free suits a people who, for centuries, lived by moving with their herds across one of the largest grasslands on Earth.
