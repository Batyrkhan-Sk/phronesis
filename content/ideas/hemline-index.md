The idea is appealing: in **good times** people feel daring and skirts get **shorter**, as with the flappers of the 1920s and the miniskirts of the 1960s. In **bad times** they get **longer**, as in the 1930s Depression.

## The wrong economist

The theory is almost always attributed to the economist **George Taylor** in **1926**. But Taylor’s thesis was about the growth of the **hosiery** industry, and didn’t propose a hemline theory. One source suggests the idea came from **Paul Nystrom’s** 1928 book *The Economics of Fashion*. Like many good stories, it seems to have gathered a more impressive origin as it was retold.

## Does it work?

In **2010**, two Dutch economists, **van Baardwijk and Franses**, compared hemline lengths since 1921 with economic data. They found something, but not the classic claim: if anything, the **economy leads hemlines by about three years**. Their work wasn’t peer-reviewed.

Even if there is a link, the causes are probably mixed: fabric prices, war-time rationing, new materials, designers and celebrities, and the simple fact that fashion has to keep changing to sell.

**A useful takeaway:** when someone shows you a neat indicator with a famous origin, check both the origin and the data. Here, neither holds up well.
