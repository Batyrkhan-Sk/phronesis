Order dessert in a restaurant in Beijing and you won’t be offered a fortune cookie. The cookie is **American**, and its ancestor is **Japanese**.

## The Kyoto cracker

In the 19th century, bakeries near Kyoto made **tsujiura senbei**: folded crackers with a fortune slip. In the Japanese version the slip was tucked into the **outside bend**, not baked inside, and the cracker was flavoured with sesame and miso rather than vanilla.

## California claims

Several people claimed to have introduced the modern cookie to America:

- **Makoto Hagiwara** of the **Japanese Tea Garden** in San Francisco’s Golden Gate Park is reported to have served them in the early 1900s.
- **David Jung**, founder of a Los Angeles noodle company, claimed he invented it in **1918**.
- **Seiichi Kito** of the Fugetsu-do bakery in Los Angeles also claimed credit.

In **1983**, San Francisco’s semi-serious **Court of Historical Review** held a mock trial and ruled for Hagiwara. A fortune cookie entered as evidence read: *“S.F. Judge who rules for L.A. Not Very Smart Cookie.”*

## How it became “Chinese”

During **World War II**, Japanese-Americans, including many bakers, were forced into internment camps. **Chinese-American** manufacturers took over production, and the cookie became a fixture of Chinese restaurants across the US. The writer **Jennifer 8. Lee**, working with the Japanese researcher Yasuko Nakamachi, traced this history in *The Fortune Cookie Chronicles*.
