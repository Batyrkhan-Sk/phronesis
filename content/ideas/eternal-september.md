Before the web, many people met online through **Usenet**: a worldwide system of discussion groups, run by volunteers and universities, that began around 1980. It had customs of its own, such as reading a group for a while before posting and not asking questions that its FAQ had already answered.

## The yearly September

Each **September**, universities started a new year and thousands of students got internet access for the first time. They posted clumsily, broke etiquette and asked the same questions again. Regulars grumbled, explained the rules, and by about October most newcomers had learnt them or drifted away. The cycle was familiar enough to have a name: the **September influx**.

## 1993: it didn’t stop

In **September 1993**, **America Online** gave its customers a way into Usenet. These were ordinary home users arriving in huge numbers, all year round, not just a class of students. Existing users could no longer teach them in time to keep up. The newcomers weren’t doing anything wrong, but the community’s way of absorbing people had been swamped.

In **January 1994**, a user called **Dave Fischer** wrote on the group alt.folklore.computers that September 1993 would be remembered as the September that never ended. The phrase **Eternal September** spread, and it has since become shorthand for any online community changed by a sudden flood of new members.

## How to tell it fairly

It is tempting to treat it as a story of good old days spoiled by outsiders. That is an **interpretation**, not a settled fact. Usenet had many problems of its own, and the new arrivals eventually became its culture. A kinder lesson is that communities have to teach their norms deliberately, and that every old hand was once a confused newcomer.
