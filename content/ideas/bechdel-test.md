Here is a surprisingly useful question to ask about almost any film: **do two women, who have names, talk to each other about something other than a man?**

That is the whole **Bechdel test**. It is named after the cartoonist **Alison Bechdel**, but she is quick to say the idea was not hers.

## A joke in an alternative newspaper

The test appears in a 1985 episode of her long-running strip *Dykes to Watch Out For*, titled “The Rule”. One character explains that she will only see a film if it has at least two women in it, who talk to each other, about something besides a man. Bechdel has described it as a little joke in an alternative feminist paper, and credits her friend **Liz Wallace**, which is why she has said she would rather it were called the **Bechdel–Wallace test**.

Bechdel has also said she believes Wallace may have been inspired by **Virginia Woolf**, who noted in *A Room of One’s Own* how rarely women in fiction are shown as friends. That link is a reasonable reading rather than a documented fact.

## What it does, and doesn’t, tell you

The test took off decades later, in the 2000s, and some Swedish cinemas and a TV channel even used it in their ratings in 2013. Its strength is that it is simple and easy to check. Its weakness is exactly the same.

- **It is a low bar.** Many films pass while giving women very little to do.
- **It is not a quality score.** Plenty of fine films fail, and some films centred on a single woman fail by design.
- **It measures a pattern, not a verdict.** Asked about thousands of films, it shows how rarely women’s conversations get screen time.

Bechdel herself treats it as light social commentary, not a scientific standard. That is the generous way to use it too: as a curious lens that makes a favourite film look new, not as a stick to beat anyone with.

## Names matter

Variants add rules: both women must be named, or the conversation must last a minimum time. Each variant shows the same lesson, that a simple rule is easy to game and easy to apply, so it is best used as a starting question rather than an answer.
