The idea behind **double entry** is simple: every transaction is recorded **twice**, as a debit in one account and a credit in another. Selling goods for cash increases cash and decreases inventory. Because the two sides must always balance, errors and fraud become much easier to spot.

Italian merchants were using it by the 13th and 14th centuries. Surviving Florentine and Genoese ledgers show it. In **1494**, the Franciscan friar **Luca Pacioli** described the “method of Venice” in his *Summa de arithmetica*, one of the first printed mathematics books. The printing press carried it across Europe.

## The Leonardo connection

Pacioli lived in Milan with **Leonardo da Vinci** and taught him mathematics. Leonardo, in return, drew the remarkable polyhedra for Pacioli’s *De divina proportione* (1509), a book about the [golden ratio](idea:golden-ratio). So the friar is linked both to the Renaissance image of perfect proportion and to the balance sheet.

## Why it matters

Double entry made it possible to see a business as **a single entity separate from its owner**, with assets, debts and profit that can be measured. Some historians link that abstraction to the growth of joint-stock companies and capitalism itself. That link is an interpretation, but the influence of double-entry bookkeeping on how organisations think about money is hard to overstate.
