In the early 1900s, crowds in Berlin gathered to watch a horse called **Hans**. His owner, a man named **Wilhelm von Osten**, would ask him a sum, and Hans would tap out the answer with a hoof. He seemed to handle addition, subtraction and more.

## Not a swindle

Von Osten appears to have sincerely believed his horse could think. In 1904 a panel of 13 people led by the psychologist **Carl Stumpf** examined Hans and found no deliberate trickery. That left a puzzle: if nobody was cheating, how was it done?

## The experiment that solved it

Stumpf’s student **Oskar Pfungst** ran controlled tests. He found that Hans failed when the questioner did not know the answer, and when Hans could not see the questioner. In one account, Hans was right about 89% of the time when he could see someone who knew the answer, and about 6% of the time when he could not.

Hans had learnt something real, but not arithmetic. As he tapped, the people around him unconsciously **tensed up**, and relaxed when he reached the right number. Pfungst found they were giving a cue without knowing it, and Hans stopped at the relief.

## Why it matters

The **Clever Hans effect** now names a hazard in any test where the person running it knows the answer: they may steer the result without meaning to. It is part of the reason for **double-blind** studies, where neither tester nor subject knows which condition is which. Researchers on animal minds, and people evaluating AI systems, still use it as a warning.

It is also a gentle story. Hans was smart, just at something else: he read people better than anyone had realised. Von Osten, for his part, never accepted Pfungst’s explanation.

## A word of care

The “89% and 6%” figures come from popular summaries of Pfungst’s work, so quote them as approximate. The commission also did not settle whether the horse could reason; Stumpf’s own introduction to Pfungst’s book says it left that question open, and it was Pfungst’s later experiments that answered it.
