Look up on a clear night and you see mostly black. That is odd, and people have noticed for over four hundred years.

## The puzzle

Imagine an infinite universe, evenly filled with stars that have shone forever. Wherever you look, your line of sight would eventually hit the surface of a star. A star farther away looks fainter, but there are more of them, so the two effects cancel. The whole sky should be as bright as the surface of a star.

## Not really Olbers’ idea

The paradox carries the name of the German astronomer **Heinrich Olbers**, who wrote about it in the 1820s, but he was not first. **Kepler** used the dark sky around 1610 as an argument against an infinite universe of stars. **Halley** and **Chéseaux** studied it in the 18th century. Their favourite fix, that dust or a thin medium absorbs the light, does not work: as **John Herschel** pointed out, absorbing dust would warm up until it shone too.

## A poet gets close

In 1848 **Edgar Allan Poe** wrote *Eureka*, a prose poem about the cosmos. He suggested that the sky is dark because the farthest stars are so far that their light has not yet reached us. It was not a scientific paper, and he gave no proof, but it points in the right direction.

## The answer

The modern answer is that the universe is **finite in age**, and the stars have not shone forever. Light from stars beyond a certain distance has simply not had time to arrive. **Redshift** from the expanding universe also dims distant light, but the astronomer Edward Harrison showed it is a smaller part of the story than the finite age of the stars.

## Why it makes a good dinner topic

It starts from something anyone can see tonight, with no equipment. It shows that a simple observation can carry a large conclusion: the dark of the night sky is a clue that the universe has a history.
