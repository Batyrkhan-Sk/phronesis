Buy a red car and red cars seem to appear everywhere. Learn the word “sonder” and you will meet it twice this week. This happens to almost everyone, and it has two parts:

1. **Selective attention.** Your brain filters out most of what you see. Once something becomes *relevant*, it gets through the filter.
2. **Confirmation bias.** Every new sighting feels like evidence that the thing really has become more common. You don’t count the days you didn’t see it.

## Where the name comes from

In 1994 a reader of the *St. Paul Pioneer Press* wrote in to say that after first hearing of the **Baader–Meinhof** group (a West German militant organisation), he heard it mentioned again within 24 hours. Other readers wrote in with similar experiences, and the name stuck online. In 2005 the Stanford linguist **Arnold Zwicky** gave it the more descriptive name **frequency illusion**.

## Why it’s useful to know

- **Learning:** it is a good sign. Your brain has started indexing the new idea.
- **Health anxiety:** after reading about a symptom, you will notice it more. That is the illusion, not necessarily the illness.
- **Marketing:** advertisers rely on it. A single exposure makes later encounters stand out.

This platform is, in a way, designed to produce the frequency illusion on purpose.
