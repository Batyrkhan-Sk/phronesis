The **Gregorian calendar** follows the **sun**: 365 or 366 days, keeping the seasons in place. The **Islamic calendar** follows the **moon**: twelve lunar months, a year of **354 or 355 days**.

## The 11-day drift

Because the lunar year is about **11 days shorter**, every Islamic date arrives about 11 days **earlier** each year in the Gregorian calendar. **Ramadan**, the month of fasting from dawn to sunset, moves through the seasons:

- In some decades it falls in **summer**, with long, hot days of fasting.
- About 15 years later it falls in **winter**, with short days.
- After about **33 years** it is back where it started.

So someone who fasts for most of their life experiences Ramadan in every season, more than once.

## Watching for the moon

Traditionally, each month begins with the **sighting of the new crescent moon** (*hilal*). Clouds, location and different religious authorities mean that **Ramadan and Eid can begin on different days in different countries**, which is why the start date is often announced only the night before. Some communities now use astronomical calculations instead.

## Counting from the Hijra

Year 1 of the calendar starts with the **Hijra**, Muhammad’s migration from Mecca to Medina in **622 CE**. Years are marked **AH**, *Anno Hegirae*. In much of the Muslim world, the Hijri calendar is used for religious life alongside the Gregorian calendar for civil purposes.
