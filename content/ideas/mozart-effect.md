You may have heard that playing Mozart to a baby will make the child smarter. It is one of the most repeated claims in popular psychology, and it is built on very little.

## What the experiment found

In 1993 Frances Rauscher, Gordon Shaw and Katherine Ky published a short paper in the journal **Nature**. University students listened to ten minutes of a Mozart sonata, to relaxation instructions, or to silence, and then did a spatial reasoning task. After the Mozart, scores were slightly higher. The gain faded within about **10 to 15 minutes**, and it applied to that one kind of task.

There were no babies, and no claim about general intelligence.

## How it grew

The press shortened the story to “Mozart makes you smarter”. Products and CDs followed, and in the late 1990s the idea reached public policy: US officials in at least one state moved to give classical music to newborns. The label **“Mozart effect”** stuck.

## What later research says

A large **2010 meta-analysis** by Pietschnig, Voracek and Formann pooled many studies. It found little evidence for an effect specific to Mozart, and signs that the results were stronger in some labs and affected by publication bias. A more modest explanation is that music you enjoy can lift your **mood and alertness**, which helps a little for a little while. That would hold for other music, and for a good coffee.

## The kinder takeaway

None of this means music is pointless. Learning to play an instrument is a different matter and is studied separately. The honest line is that Mozart is wonderful for its own sake, and that you do not need a study to justify it.
