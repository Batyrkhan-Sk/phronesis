Walk into any shop selling baby clothes and the signal is clear: **pink for girls, blue for boys**. It feels timeless. It is not.

## Before the code

Around **1900**, babies and toddlers of both sexes were usually dressed in **white**, often in dresses. The practical reason is easy to appreciate: white cotton could be bleached hot and often. Boys commonly kept their dresses and long hair until about the age of six.

## The 1918 surprise

Pastel colours for babies began appearing in the mid-19th century, but nobody agreed which was which. In **June 1918**, the trade publication ***Earnshaw’s Infants’ Department*** told retailers that the generally accepted rule was **pink for boys and blue for girls**. The reasoning was that pink, being stronger, suited a boy, while blue, being more delicate and dainty, suited a girl. Other advice at the time suggested colours by hair or eye colour instead, such as blue for blondes and pink for brunettes.

Historian **Jo Paoletti**, who has studied this period closely, notes that US shops in the 1920s did not agree with each other either; some leading department stores advised pink for boys.

## How it settled

The modern pairing seems to have hardened around the **1940s**, as retailers and manufacturers pushed one clear rule that was easy to sell. That date is an interpretation of retail history rather than a single documented moment. In the **late 1960s and 1970s**, unisex clothing was fashionable and the colours faded in importance. From the **mid-1980s** they came back more intensely, which Paoletti connects to prenatal testing: parents who knew a baby’s sex in advance wanted to buy for it.

## A friendly takeaway

None of this says people should dress children one way or another. It simply shows that a custom can feel ancient while being only a few generations old, which makes it a gentle, curious topic rather than a quarrel.
