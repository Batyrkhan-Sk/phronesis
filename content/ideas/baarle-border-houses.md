Most borders are lines across fields and rivers. In **Baarle** the line is more like a puzzle. The town is split between **Baarle-Hertog** in Belgium and **Baarle-Nassau** in the Netherlands, and the two are woven together street by street.

## A patchwork

Baarle-Hertog is not one block of land. It is made of **many separate Belgian parcels** scattered inside the Netherlands. Some are tiny, and a few have little Dutch parcels inside them, so there are places that are Belgium, inside the Netherlands, inside Belgium. Sources give slightly different totals, since it depends on how you count the pieces, but “about two dozen Belgian parcels” is a safe description.

## The front door rule

When the border cuts through a house, which country does the house belong to? The answer is simple: **wherever the front door is**. Many houses show a house number in the national colours of the country they belong to. A building can even have a Belgian address on one side and a Dutch address on another.

## How it got like this

Nobody designed it. The pattern comes from **feudal land grants**: the **Duke of Brabant** gave some parcels to his vassals and kept others, while neighbouring land belonged to the **lords of Breda**. When Belgium and the Netherlands were separated, the **1843 Treaty of Maastricht** set the border but left Baarle’s tangle largely as it was, and later commissions and agreements in the 20th century worked on it parcel by parcel.

## A good thing to bring up

It is a fun reminder that a country’s border is a legal fact, not a natural one. For the people who live there, it mostly matters for paperwork such as taxes, postal services and which authority responds to what.
