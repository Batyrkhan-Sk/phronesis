Vienna General Hospital had two maternity clinics. The **First Clinic**, staffed by doctors and medical students, had a death rate from **childbed fever** of around **10%**. The **Second Clinic**, staffed by midwives, averaged under **4%**. Women knew it, and some begged not to be admitted to the first.

## The clue

In 1847 Semmelweis’s friend **Jakob Kolletschka** was cut by a student’s scalpel during an autopsy and died with symptoms very like childbed fever. The difference between the clinics clicked: doctors went **straight from dissecting corpses** to examining women in labour. Midwives didn’t do autopsies.

## The experiment

From **mid-May 1847**, Semmelweis required doctors to wash their hands in **chlorinated lime**. Deaths in the First Clinic fell from **18.3%** in April to **2.2%** in June and **1.2%** in July.

## The legend and the history

The popular version: the establishment mocked him, he was driven out, and he died in an asylum. Parts of that are true. He left Vienna in 1849, his main book appeared only in **1861**, and he died in an asylum in **1865**, aged 47, probably from a wound after a beating by guards.

But historians, and some sharp discussion threads online, add nuance. **Many doctors did adopt handwashing**; what they rejected was his claim that *all* childbed fever had a single cause. Semmelweis also published late and attacked critics personally, which hurt his case. Germ theory, which explained *why* he was right, arrived only with Pasteur and Lister in the 1860s.

**A useful takeaway:** being right isn’t enough. The evidence needs explaining and communicating. That lesson, rather than “experts are fools”, is the one that holds up.
