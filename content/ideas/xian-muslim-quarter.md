Behind Xi’an’s Drum Tower, a few minutes from the city centre, the street signs, the white caps and the smell of grilled lamb tell you you’ve entered the **Muslim Quarter**, home for centuries to the **Hui** people.

## Who the Hui are

The Hui are **Chinese-speaking Muslims**, about **11.3 million** people, the most widely spread minority in China. Their ancestry combines **Han Chinese** with **Arab, Persian and Central Asian** traders and settlers who came along the Silk Road from the Tang dynasty onwards, and especially under the Mongol Yuan. Over generations they married locally, spoke Chinese and kept their faith.

Hui food follows Islamic dietary law, so it is **pork-free** in a country where pork is the most common meat. That has given Xi’an a distinctive street-food culture of **lamb and beef**.

## A mosque that looks like a temple

The **Great Mosque of Xi’an** was founded, according to tradition, in **742**. Its current buildings date mostly from **1392**, in the Ming dynasty, and they look Chinese: courtyards, carved wooden gates and tiled roofs with upturned eaves, and **no dome**. Its tower, the “Examining the Heart Tower”, is a three-storey **octagonal pagoda** that probably once served as the minaret.

The complex covers about **1.2 hectares** and is still an active place of worship.

## Why it matters

Xi’an is often presented only through emperors and the Terracotta Army. Its Muslim Quarter is a reminder that the Silk Road wasn’t just a route for goods. It was also how **people** arrived and settled, and their descendants still live there.
