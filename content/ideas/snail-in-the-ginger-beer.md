On **26 August 1928** a woman visited a café in Paisley, near Glasgow, with a friend. The friend paid for a ginger beer for her. It came in a **dark, opaque bottle**, so nobody could see inside. As the bottle was topped up, Mrs Donoghue said, the remains of a **decomposed snail** slid out. She became ill with gastroenteritis and shock.

## A gap in the law

This was a real problem. Contract law links the person who *pays* with the person who is owed something. The friend had bought the drink, so Mrs Donoghue had no contract with the café, and none with the manufacturer. Existing law offered her little. Her lawyers argued something bolder: that a manufacturer can be **negligent** towards the person who ends up using the product, even without any contract.

## The neighbour principle

In **1932** the House of Lords agreed, by **three votes to two**. **Lord Atkin** drew on a moral idea most people know, that you should love your neighbour, and turned it into a legal test: you must take reasonable care not to injure those you can reasonably foresee would be affected by what you do. Courts in Scotland, England and many other countries went on to use this **duty of care** as the starting point for negligence claims.

## The twist

Here is the part that makes people sit up: the Lords decided only a **question of law**. They assumed her story was true for the purposes of the appeal. There was never a trial where the snail was proved, and Stevenson died before the case could be heard. It later settled, for a sum sources give as about £100, although the exact figure is uncertain. Some modern lawyers and historians have raised doubts about the incident itself; those are doubts, not findings.

**Why it matters:** the law of negligence, which affects everything from faulty products to medical care, grew from one café visit and a legal argument about what we owe to people we will never meet.
