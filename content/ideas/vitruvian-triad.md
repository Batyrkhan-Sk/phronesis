If you had to judge a building in three questions, which would you ask?

## The Roman original

**Vitruvius** was a Roman author who wrote a ten-book treatise, *De architectura*, dedicated to the emperor Augustus. It is the only architectural treatise to survive from the ancient world. Dating his life precisely is difficult, but the work is usually placed around 30–20 BC.

In Book I he says a building should have three qualities: **firmitas**, **utilitas** and **venustas**. In plain terms: it should **stand up**, **work for the people using it**, and **be beautiful**.

## How it became poetry

The version most English speakers know comes from **Henry Wotton**, an English diplomat, whose 1624 book *The Elements of Architecture* gave **“firmness, commodity and delight”**. Here *commodity* means convenience, not goods for sale. The phrase has often been quoted in that form, and Wotton’s exact ordering and phrasing are a free-ish rendering; whether it is a faithful translation is a matter of reading.

## Why it keeps working

The triad is useful because the three can **pull against one another**. A strong, beautiful palace may be wretched to live in. A convenient shed may not please anyone. A daring design may be lovely and leak.

You can test it on anything: a station, a school, a bus shelter. Which leg is weakest? That question is often more interesting than the argument about whether a building is “nice”. It also makes arguments about styles fairer. People who dislike [Brutalism](idea:brutalism) are usually judging the third leg, whereas its defenders are often pointing to the first two.

## A caution

Vitruvius is not an infallible guide. He wrote for Rome and for patrons, and later architects reinterpreted him heavily after the treatise was widely read again in the fifteenth century. Treat the triad as a **prompt for conversation**, not a rulebook.
