## Model 1: trickle down (1904)

The sociologist **Georg Simmel** saw fashion as a contest between two urges: to **fit in** and to **stand out**. The upper classes adopt a new style to show their status. The middle classes **imitate** them. Once a style is common, the elite **abandon** it for something new, and the chase starts again.

The economist **Thorstein Veblen** (1899) added the idea of **conspicuous consumption**: expensive, impractical clothes are a way of displaying wealth. Corsets and long trains showed that you didn’t do manual work.

## Model 2: trickle across, and bubble up

By the 1960s, mass production and magazines put new styles in front of every class **at the same time**. Researchers found that fashions mostly spread **sideways**, through opinion leaders within each group. Meanwhile many important styles moved **upward**: jeans (workwear), leather jackets, punk, hip-hop streetwear and sneakers all began outside the elite, though they usually became mainstream only after designers and celebrities adopted them.

## Model 3: the algorithm

Social media changed the speed. On TikTok, a look can be named, filmed, copied by thousands and sold by fast-fashion brands within days. Journalists and fashion writers describe:

- **“-core” aesthetics**: cottagecore, balletcore, “clean girl”, Barbiecore. These are bundles of clothes, colours, food and interiors with a name that makes them easy to search and copy.
- **Microtrends**: items that peak and fade within **weeks or months**, where trends once took years.

There isn’t yet solid academic measurement of how much faster cycles have become, so treat the specific numbers as **reporting, not established fact**. The underlying mechanism is Simmel’s, sped up: people imitate to belong and move on to stand out. Algorithms simply supply far more people to imitate.
