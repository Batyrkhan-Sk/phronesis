In **March 1974**, during a drought, farmers in Lintong, near Xi’an, were digging a **well**. About 1.5 km from a large earthen mound, they hit fragments of fired clay: a head, then pieces of bodies.

They had found the **Terracotta Army**.

## An army for the afterlife

The figures were made for **Qin Shi Huang**, who unified China in 221 BC and became its **first emperor**. Three pits excavated so far hold, by a 2007 estimate, **more than 8,000 soldiers**, **130 chariots** with **520 horses**, and **150 cavalry horses**.

The faces were built from around **ten basic moulds** and then individually modelled, so no two soldiers look quite the same.

## They were colourful

Today the army is earth-brown. Originally it was **painted**: red from cinnabar and iron oxide, white from burnt bone, and many other pigments. In Xi’an’s dry air, exposed paint can **flake off in minutes**, which is one reason excavation is slow.

## The tomb nobody has opened

The army guards the emperor’s **burial mound**, which has **never been excavated**.

About a century after the burial, the historian **Sima Qian** described the tomb: a model of the empire with **rivers of mercury**, kept flowing by machinery, and crossbows set to fire at intruders. It sounded like legend, until soil tests on the mound found **unusually high levels of mercury**.

Archaeologists have decided not to open it, at least for now. The usual reasons given are preservation (they don’t yet know how to protect what’s inside, as the fading paint shows) and respect for the site. It remains one of the most significant unopened tombs in the world.
