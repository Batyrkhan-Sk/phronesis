When you sing **do, re, mi**, you are quoting a prayer.

## The hymn

“**Ut queant laxis**” is a Latin hymn to **St John the Baptist**, written in a verse form called Sapphic. The text is traditionally credited to **Paul the Deacon**, an eighth-century Lombard historian, though that is not certain. Roughly translated, it asks the saint to relieve the singers’ tired lips so they can sing his praise.

Look at the first syllable of each half-line:

- **Ut** queant laxis
- **Re**sonare fibris
- **Mi**ra gestorum
- **Fa**muli tuorum
- **Sol**ve polluti
- **La**bii reatum

Each one is sung one step higher than the last.

## Guido’s trick

The monk and teacher **Guido of Arezzo**, in the early 11th century, is credited with noticing this and giving singers a memory aid: learn the tune, and you carry the pitch of each syllable with you. Whether he wrote the melody is unclear. Many scholars think he used an existing one.

## From Ut to Do

- **Ut → Do.** “Ut” is awkward to sing, so singers began using “Do” instead, in the 17th century. A popular account credits **Giovanni Battista Doni** and says he took the syllable from his own surname. That is a good story, but treat it as tradition rather than proof.
- **Si.** The seventh note is commonly explained as the initials of the hymn’s last words, *Sancte Iohannes*.
- **Ti.** English speakers changed *si* to *ti* in the 19th century (credited to **Sarah Glover**) so that no two syllables start with the same letter.
