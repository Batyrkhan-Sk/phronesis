Imagine you are about to buy a phone, and the maker announces that a much better one arrives next month. You wait. Multiply that by thousands of customers and you have the **Osborne effect**: a company’s early talk about its future product drains the sales of the one it sells today.

## The company behind the name

**Adam Osborne**’s company made the **Osborne 1**, an early portable computer. In early 1983 Osborne showed its more advanced successor, the **Osborne Executive**, to journalists. According to accounts from the time, dealers heard about it and **cancelled their orders** for the machine they already stocked. The company cut prices sharply, but sales did not recover, and it filed for bankruptcy on **13 September 1983**.

## Did talking really do it?

This is the part worth repeating carefully. The tidy story, “they announced too soon and died”, is a **disputed** one. The technology writer **Robert X. Cringely** and others argue that the firm was already in trouble: it had built more Osborne 1 machines than it could sell, its management was stretched and its cash was running short. The announcement may have made things worse, but it may not have been the cause.

So the Osborne effect is real as a pattern, and a company named after it may be an unfair example.

## Why it still matters

Firms today walk a line between building excitement and holding back sales. That is why product launches are often tightly secret until shortly before release, and why leaks are treated so seriously. For anyone listening to a business story, the lesson is two-fold: a good name for a pattern is useful, and a single famous example deserves a second look.

## A story that fits too neatly

Named effects spread because they explain a failure in one sentence. The risk is that the sentence replaces the evidence. With Osborne, even the sources that accept the cancelled-orders story add that overproduction and money problems were present too, so it is fairest to say the announcement was a trigger or an accelerant, and that historians still weigh how much it counted.
