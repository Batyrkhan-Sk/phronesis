In the early Soviet film school, film stock was scarce, so **Lev Kuleshov** experimented by re-editing existing footage.

He took a neutral close-up of the popular actor **Ivan Mozzhukhin** and cut it after three different shots:

1. A bowl of **soup** → audiences saw **hunger**
2. A **child in a coffin** → audiences saw **grief**
3. A **woman on a divan** → audiences saw **desire**

According to accounts by Kuleshov and his student Vsevolod Pudovkin, viewers praised the actor’s subtle performance. The face was **identical** each time.

## Why it matters

Kuleshov drew the conclusion that **meaning in cinema comes from the juxtaposition of shots**, not from the shots alone. This became the foundation of Soviet montage theory (Eisenstein and others), and Hitchcock described it in interviews. A reaction shot of an actor watching something doesn’t need much acting. The cut does much of the work.

## Did it really happen?

The original film is **lost**, and descriptions vary. Modern replications have had mixed results. A 1992 attempt found little effect, while later studies with better designs (including eye-tracking and brain imaging) found that **context measurably shifts which emotion people perceive**. The effect is real but less dramatic than the legend.
