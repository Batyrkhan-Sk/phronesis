Charles Babbage first designed a **Difference Engine**, a huge calculator for producing error-free mathematical tables. Then, in 1837, he described something much more ambitious.

The **Analytical Engine** was to be programmable. Its parts map surprisingly well onto a modern computer:

| Babbage’s term | Modern equivalent |
| --- | --- |
| The Store | Memory |
| The Mill | Processor (CPU) |
| Punched cards | Program and input |
| Printer and bell | Output |

It could also make **decisions**, jumping to different instructions depending on a result. That conditional branching is what makes a calculator a computer.

## Why it was never built

Funding disputes, the precision engineering required, and Babbage’s habit of redesigning everything all got in the way. When he died in 1871, only fragments existed.

There is a modern postscript. In 1991, the bicentenary of Babbage’s birth, London’s Science Museum built his **Difference Engine No. 2** from the original drawings using tolerances available in his era. It worked, which suggests the problem was never the idea.
