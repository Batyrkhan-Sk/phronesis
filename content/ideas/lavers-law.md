**James Laver** was a curator of costume at the **Victoria and Albert Museum** in London. In *Taste and Fashion* (1937) he compressed the life of a style into one timeline:

| When | How the style looks |
| --- | --- |
| 10 years before its time | Indecent |
| 5 years before | Shameless |
| 1 year before | Daring |
| **Current** | **Smart** |
| 1 year after | Dowdy |
| 10 years after | Hideous |
| 20 years after | Ridiculous |
| 30 years after | Amusing |
| 50 years after | Quaint |
| 70 years after | Charming |
| 100 years after | Romantic |
| 150 years after | Beautiful |

## Why it rings true

The worst moment for a style is **10–20 years** after it peaks, when it’s associated with people just older than you: your parents, your older siblings, your own embarrassing photos. Once that generation has moved on, the style loses its associations and can become **amusing**, then **retro**, then something to revive.

That’s why fashion keeps “rediscovering” decades about **20–30 years** back: the 1970s in the 1990s, the 1990s in the 2010s, and the 2000s (“Y2K”) in the 2020s.

## Take it lightly

Laver never offered it as a scientific law, and fashion historians have criticised his theories as oversimplified. The exact numbers don’t hold, and today’s cycles are much faster. But the **shape** of the curve, with repulsion first and nostalgia later, is a useful mental model for clothes, music, furniture, fonts and slang.
