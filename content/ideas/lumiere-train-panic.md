The picture is vivid: Paris, 1896, a darkened room, and a locomotive that seems to steam straight out of the screen. The audience shrieks, leaps up and scrambles for the doors.

It is told in almost every introduction to film. It is also very probably **not what happened**.

## The film

*L’Arrivée d’un train en gare de La Ciotat* is a single shot of roughly **50 seconds**, made by the brothers **Auguste and Louis Lumière**. A train pulls in at a station on the French Riviera and passengers step down. The camera sits low and near the track, so the engine grows large on the screen. The Lumières began selling tickets to Cinématographe shows in Paris on **28 December 1895**, and the film was part of their repertoire.

## Where is the panic?

The film scholar **Martin Loiperdinger** went looking for evidence in a 2004 essay called “Lumière’s Arrival of the Train: Cinema’s Founding Myth”. He found no police reports, newspaper accounts or diaries from the time describing a crowd in flight. What the sources do show is an audience that was **amazed**, which is not the same thing as terrified.

The panic story appears later, in film histories and memoirs, and it grew with retelling. A British comedy from **1901**, *The Countryman and the Cinematograph*, already jokes about a rustic who flees from a film, so the idea of the naive viewer was circulating within a few years.

## A better story to tell

It is true that early audiences were thrilled by moving photographs. A plausible reading is that the legend dramatises that sense of wonder, and that it flatters later audiences, who get to feel more sophisticated than their great-grandparents. That last part is an **interpretation**, not a documented fact.

So the generous version for dinner is this: people in 1896 almost certainly knew a picture of a train could not run them over. They were amazed anyway, and that is the real story.

## Which film is it?

The Lumières shot more than one version of the scene, and the one most often seen online has well-dressed passengers in the foreground, which appears to be a staged, later take. The first-night programme is also hard to reconstruct, which is another reason to be careful with claims such as “the audience at the very first screening…”.
