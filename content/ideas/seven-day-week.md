Almost everything about how we count time comes from the sky. A **day** is one turn of the Earth. A **year** is one trip round the Sun. A **month** comes from the Moon’s cycle of about 29.5 days. The **week** is the odd one out: nothing astronomical lasts seven days, and 29.5 doesn’t divide neatly into sevens.

## Two roots

The seven-day week seems to come from two separate traditions that merged.

**The Sabbath week.** In the Jewish tradition, followed later by Christianity and Islam in their own ways, time is counted in cycles of six days of work and a seventh of rest. Some scholars have looked for a Babylonian ancestor of the Sabbath, but that link is **debated**, so it’s safer to say the origin is uncertain.

**The planetary week.** The ancient world knew seven wandering bodies: the **Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus and Saturn**. Greco-Roman astrologers assigned them to days, and the habit appears in the Roman world around the end of the first century BC.

## Names that survived

The planetary week is why Saturday is **Saturn’s day** and why French still says *lundi*, “Moon day”. English Tuesday to Friday took the same pattern but swapped Roman gods for Germanic equivalents: **Tiw** for Mars, **Woden** for Mercury, **Thor** for Jupiter and **Frigg** for Venus.

## Where Constantine comes in

A common claim is that the emperor Constantine created the week. He didn’t. By his time, counting in sevens was already general across the empire. In **321** he made Sunday an official rest day for townspeople and courts, which helped fix a pattern that already existed.

## A week that didn’t stick

Revolutionary France replaced the week with a **ten-day** cycle in 1793 to make the calendar rational, and it was dropped within years. The seven-day week outlasted it because people were used to it, which may be the best argument that a calendar is a social agreement as much as an astronomical fact.
