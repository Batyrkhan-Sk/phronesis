Most cities solve a drainage problem by digging. Chicago went the other way and **lifted the city**.

## The problem: a city that couldn’t drain

Chicago grew fast in the mid-19th century on ground that was barely above the level of Lake Michigan. Rain and waste had nowhere to go, streets turned to mud, and epidemic diseases such as typhoid and cholera were a constant fear.

In 1856 the engineer **Ellis Chesbrough** produced a plan for a proper sewer system. To make the drains work by gravity, the streets had to be higher than the lake. So the city ordered the **grade** (the street level) raised, by amounts that varied from street to street and are often quoted at roughly four to fourteen feet.

## Then the buildings had to follow

Raising the street left shops and hotels sitting in a pit. Their owners had a choice: rebuild, or lift the building. Teams of workers placed hundreds or thousands of **jackscrews** beneath the walls and turned them a little at a time, in careful unison, while masons built new foundations underneath.

The first large masonry buildings were raised in 1858. In one famous lift a whole half-block of shops on Lake Street was raised in a few days.

## The hotel that stayed open

The best-known story is the **Tremont House**, a large hotel raised while guests were still inside. Accounts say people carried on eating and sleeping while the building rose around them. The young **George Pullman** worked on lifts like this before he became rich making sleeping cars for railways.

Be careful with the numbers. Sources disagree on the year of the Tremont lift (1858 or 1861) and on how many jackscrews and men were used, so it’s safest to say “hundreds of men and thousands of jackscrews” unless you know which account you’re quoting.

## Why it’s a good story

It shows a city choosing an astonishing engineering fix to a very unglamorous problem, and a reminder that the ground under a famous skyline was once deliberately moved.
