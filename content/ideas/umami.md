Western science long recognised **four** basic tastes: sweet, sour, salty and bitter. **Kikunae Ikeda**, a chemist at Tokyo Imperial University, was sure there was another one. It was the deep, savoury taste of **dashi**, the Japanese broth made from **kombu** seaweed.

In **1908** he worked out what caused it: **glutamate**, an amino acid. He named its taste **umami**, from *umai*, “delicious”. A popular account says the question came to him over a bowl of his wife’s soup.

## From lab to shelf

In **1909** Ikeda and the businessman Saburōsuke Suzuki founded **Ajinomoto** and sold **monosodium glutamate (MSG)**, the world’s first umami seasoning. Other researchers found more umami substances: **inosinate** in dried bonito flakes (1913) and **guanylate** in shiitake mushrooms (1957). They also found that combining them makes the taste **much stronger**, which is why dashi uses both kombu and bonito.

## The long wait for acceptance

Western scientists were sceptical for most of the century. “Umami” was formally adopted as a scientific term in **1985**, and **receptors** for it on the tongue were identified around **2000**.

## You already love it

Parmesan, ripe tomatoes, anchovies, soy sauce, mushrooms, cured ham and the ancient Roman fish sauce **garum** are all rich in umami. Many cuisines discovered it independently; Ikeda gave it a name.
