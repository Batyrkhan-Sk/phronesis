You have probably seen it work. Someone asks a forum how to do something and gets silence. Someone else confidently posts the wrong method, and within minutes a dozen people explain exactly what is wrong with it, and the right way along with it.

That is **Cunningham’s Law**, usually worded: “The best way to get the right answer on the Internet is not to ask a question, it’s to post the wrong answer.”

## Who was Cunningham?

**Ward Cunningham** is a software pioneer who built the first **wiki**, WikiWikiWeb, in 1995. The idea behind it, that anyone can fix what is on the page, is close in spirit to the law.

## The twist: he says he didn’t say it

The name comes from **Steven McGeady**, a former colleague at the company Tektronix. In 2010 he wrote, in a comment on a New York Times language blog, that Cunningham had given him this advice in the early 1980s, about what later became Usenet.

Cunningham has said he never suggested asking questions by posting wrong answers, and called it a **misquote that disproves itself by propagating through the internet**. So the law’s own history is a small demonstration of it: a half-remembered line, repeated with confidence, attached to the wrong person’s name.

## Does it work?

It is more a **folk observation** than a tested rule. There is a plausible explanation: correcting a mistake is quick, feels good and needs no commitment, while answering a question takes work. But there are costs. Deliberately posting something false can annoy people, and wrong answers can linger and be believed.

## A kinder version

You can get the same effect honestly: share your best guess and say it is a guess. “I think it’s X, but I’m not sure. What am I missing?” invites the same correcting instinct without misleading anyone.
