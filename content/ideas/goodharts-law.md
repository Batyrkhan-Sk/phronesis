The economist **Charles Goodhart**, advising the Bank of England in 1975, noticed that monetary statistics which had been reliable guides stopped behaving once the government began **targeting** them. The anthropologist **Marilyn Strathern** later summarised the idea in the sentence everyone quotes.

## It is everywhere

- **Schools** judged by test scores **teach to the test**.
- **Call centres** measured by call length **hang up on difficult customers**.
- **Software teams** measured by lines of code **write longer code**.
- **Hospitals** with waiting-time targets have kept patients in ambulances outside so the clock wouldn’t start.

The psychologist **Donald Campbell** described the same thing in social policy in the 1970s (“Campbell’s law”): the more a quantitative indicator is used for decisions, the more it gets **corrupted**.

## What to do about it

Goodhart’s law doesn’t mean “never measure”. Some useful habits:
- Use **several measures** that are hard to game together.
- Keep **judgement in the loop**. Numbers inform decisions; they shouldn’t make them.
- **Rotate or hide** the exact metric when possible.
- Ask regularly: *is this number still telling us what we think it does?*
