Where does the sky end and space begin? The popular answer is **100 kilometres up**. The more honest answer is that it is a line we drew.

## The idea behind it

The aerodynamicist **Theodore von Kármán** pointed out that the higher an aircraft flies, the thinner the air and the faster it must go to generate lift. At some altitude the speed needed to stay up is the same as the speed needed to orbit, so a wing is pointless. His calculation put that altitude at roughly **84 km (275,000 feet)**.

The line was named after him later, partly through the work of lawyer **Andrew Haley**, who was interested in where national airspace might end.

## Why 100 km?

The aviation body **FAI** adopted **100 km** for spaceflight records. It is a round figure, easy to remember and close to the original calculation. The 100 km value, rather than a physical law, is the convention in everyday use.

Meanwhile, the United States has long awarded astronaut wings for flights above **50 miles (about 80 km)**. In 2018 **Jonathan McDowell** argued that, taking the original reasoning and the behaviour of satellites at low altitude, the line is more defensible near 80 km than 100.

## No hard edge

The atmosphere fades gradually. There is no moment where the air stops. And no **treaty** sets a legal altitude for outer space, so the question of where national airspace ends remains open. In practice, countries have not challenged satellites passing over them, which has worked well enough.

## What to take away

When someone says “space starts at 100 km”, you can add: that is the standard convention, and a useful one, but it is a choice. The number came from a physics argument that was then rounded.
