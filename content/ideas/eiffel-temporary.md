The **Eiffel Tower** is so tied to the image of Paris that it feels as if it must always have been meant to stay. It wasn’t.

## A temporary landmark

The tower was built as the entrance to the **1889 World’s Fair**. Gustave Eiffel’s company held a concession of about **twenty years**, and when it ended in **1909** the monument could in principle be taken down and sold as scrap.

## “Useless and monstrous”

Before it was even finished, a letter appeared in **Le Temps** in **February 1887** from a group of artists, writers and architects, among them the novelist **Guy de Maupassant** and the architect **Charles Garnier**. They called the tower useless and monstrous and feared it would dominate the city.

You will often hear that Maupassant ate in the tower’s restaurant because it was the only place in Paris where he couldn’t see it. It’s a good line, but it is a story told about him rather than a documented fact, so tell it as an anecdote.

## How radio saved it

Eiffel knew that a monument needed a use. He supported scientific work from the tower and, in 1903, offered it to the military engineer **Captain Gustave Ferrié** for wireless telegraphy, helping to finance an antenna at the summit. By the end of the first decade of the century the tower was a valuable radio mast, and the city renewed the concession as the original term ended. Sources disagree about the length of the renewal, so avoid quoting a number.

## A good thing to take from this

The tower survived not because people suddenly decided it was beautiful, but because it turned out to be **useful** to someone else. Its aesthetic reputation caught up afterwards.
