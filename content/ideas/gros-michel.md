For the first half of the 20th century, the banana in shops in Europe and North America was the **Gros Michel** (“Big Mike”): bigger, thicker-skinned and, people said, **sweeter** than today’s banana.

## A clone army

Commercial bananas don’t grow from seeds. They are propagated from **cuttings**, so every plant of a variety is genetically **nearly identical**. That makes plantations efficient, and extremely vulnerable.

In the **1950s**, **Panama disease**, caused by the soil fungus *Fusarium oxysporum*, swept through Central American plantations. The Gros Michel had no resistance. By the **1960s** exporters had switched to the **Cavendish**, which was resistant to that strain.

## The flavour myth

A popular story, repeated in forum threads whenever bananas come up, says artificial banana flavouring tastes “wrong” because it was modelled on the Gros Michel. It’s a myth. The main flavouring compound, **isoamyl acetate**, is a simple chemical that was never based on a particular variety. It may just *happen* to be closer to the Gros Michel.

Memories of the old banana’s taste come mostly from **personal accounts**: grandparents remembering a bigger, sweeter fruit. That’s an anecdote rather than measured data, but it’s a widely shared one.

## History repeating

A new strain, **Tropical Race 4**, now attacks the Cavendish. The world again depends on a single clone. The Gros Michel is still grown in some regions free of the disease, and you can find it if you look.
