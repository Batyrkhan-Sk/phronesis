In **525**, in Rome, a monk named **Dionysius Exiguus** (“Dennis the Small”) was producing **tables for calculating Easter**. The existing tables counted years from the reign of the emperor Diocletian, a persecutor of Christians. Dionysius preferred to count from the **Incarnation**: ***Anno Domini***, “in the year of the Lord”.

## Slow to catch on

The system stayed obscure until the English monk **Bede** used it in his *Ecclesiastical History of the English People* (about **731**). From there it spread across Europe and, much later, the world. The neutral labels **CE** and **BCE** (Common Era) use the same numbering.

## No year zero

Dionysius worked before the **zero** reached Europe, so the count goes straight from **1 BC to AD 1**. That’s why the third millennium technically began on **1 January 2001**, not 2000, a point pedants enjoyed making at the time.

## A small miscalculation

The Gospel of Matthew places Jesus’s birth during the reign of **Herod the Great**, and historians date Herod’s death to around **4 BC**. So most scholars put the birth at about **6–4 BC**. Nobody knows exactly how Dionysius arrived at his figure. It just appears to have been a few years off.
