In 1995 **McArthur Wheeler** robbed two Pittsburgh banks without a disguise. He had rubbed **lemon juice** on his face, believing it would make him invisible to cameras (lemon juice can be used as invisible ink, so he reasoned it would work). When police showed him the footage, he was reportedly astonished.

The psychologist **David Dunning** read about the case and wondered whether lacking a skill also means **lacking the ability to notice that you lack it**.

## What the study found

Dunning and his student **Justin Kruger** tested people on logical reasoning, grammar and humour, then asked them to estimate how well they’d done. People in the **bottom quarter**, who scored around the 12th percentile, estimated they were around the **60th**. Top performers slightly *underestimated* themselves.

## What the internet added

The popular chart, with a spike of confidence at “Mount Stupid”, a “Valley of Despair” and a slow climb to expertise, **does not appear in the paper**. In the actual data, low performers still rated themselves **below** high performers. They were overconfident, but not *more* confident than experts.

## Is it even real?

Since 2016 several researchers have argued that much of the classic pattern appears **even with random data**. If everyone thinks they are a bit above average, and measurements are noisy, low scorers will always appear to overestimate and high scorers to underestimate. That is regression to the mean.

Dunning and others reply that the effect survives better-designed tests. The current position is roughly this: **people are poor judges of their own competence, and this matters most where feedback is rare**. Whether the poorest performers are uniquely blind is still argued over.

**A useful takeaway:** the most reliable fix for overconfidence is fast, honest feedback, from tests, peers or reality.
