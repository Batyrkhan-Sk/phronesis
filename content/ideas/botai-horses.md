On the grasslands of northern **Kazakhstan**, archaeologists found the remains of the **Botai culture**, people who lived around **3700–3100 BC**. Their settlements contained **enormous numbers of horse bones**, corral-like enclosures and thick layers of horse dung.

## Early horse-keepers

Several clues suggest Botai people did more than hunt horses:
- **wear on horse teeth** of a kind associated with **bits**, suggesting the animals were bridled;
- traces in pottery that some researchers identified as **mare’s milk**, a striking link to **kumys**, fermented mare’s milk, still popular in Kazakhstan today. That finding has since been questioned.

For years, Botai was presented as the place where **horses were first domesticated**.

## The genetic twist

In **2018**, a team sequenced ancient genomes from Botai horses. They expected to find the ancestors of modern domestic horses. Instead they found that Botai horses were mainly ancestors of **Przewalski’s horse**: the stocky, dun-coloured horse of the Mongolian steppe, famous as the **last truly wild horse** on Earth. Botai horses contributed only around **2.7%** of the ancestry of today’s domestic horses.

That suggests two things:
1. **Przewalski’s horses are probably feral**, descendants of horses that people once managed, not an untouched wild line.
2. Today’s domestic horses were mostly domesticated **somewhere else**, later.

Kazakhstan still has one of the strongest horse cultures in the world: horse meat dishes, kumys and horseback games. The steppe’s connection to horses turns out to be even older than that.
