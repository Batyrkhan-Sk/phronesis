The **Code of Hammurabi** is carved on a black basalt stele more than two metres tall. At the top, Hammurabi stands before **Shamash**, the god of justice. Below are **282 laws**. French archaeologists found it at **Susa** (in present-day Iran) in **1901**, where it had been taken as war loot centuries after it was made. It is now in the **Louvre**.

## What it covers

Most of it is not about crime. It covers **contracts, wages, rent, loans, inheritance, marriage, divorce and professional liability**. A builder whose house collapses and kills its owner can be put to death. A surgeon who botches an operation may lose a hand.

## “An eye for an eye”

Law 196: if a man destroys the eye of another man, his eye shall be destroyed. It sounds harsh, but it **caps** retaliation. You can take an eye for an eye, not a life. Many historians read such rules as an attempt to replace feuds with **proportionate, predictable** penalties.

The equality only went so far. Punishments depended on **social class**: injuring a commoner or a slave often meant paying silver, not losing an eye.

## Not the first

The **Code of Ur-Nammu** is about three centuries older. Hammurabi’s code is famous because it is so **complete and well preserved**, and because it sets out an idea we still rely on: **laws should be written down, public, and the same every time**.
