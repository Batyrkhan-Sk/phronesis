Tartan feels like the most ancient clothing in Europe. The cloth is old, but the idea that **each clan has its own pattern** is much younger than most people assume.

## Banned, then allowed

After the Jacobite defeat at Culloden, the **Dress Act of 1746** banned Highland dress, with exemptions such as the Highland regiments. Parliament repealed it in **1782**. By then, kilts and tartan were no longer everyday wear in much of the Highlands.

## Patterns acquire names

Weavers such as **Wilsons of Bannockburn** had been making tartans since the late eighteenth century, usually named after places or individuals. In **1815** the **Highland Society of London** asked clan chiefs for a signed sample of their clan tartan. A famous story says one chief went round asking the old men of his clan what the true pattern was, and no two agreed. That is hearsay, but it fits the picture.

## A king, a novelist and a book

In **1822**, **George IV** visited Edinburgh, a pageant organised by **Walter Scott**, and demand for tartan surged. Twenty years later the brothers calling themselves the **Sobieski Stuarts** published the *Vestiarium Scoticum* (1842), claiming it was based on an old manuscript. It is widely regarded as a forgery, yet many of its designs became standard clan tartans.

## So what?

None of this makes tartan less meaningful. Traditions have to start somewhere, and this one has been loved for two centuries. It is simply a good example of how a custom can feel ancient and still be recent.

## Handle with care

How much the Dress Act itself was enforced, and how many clan-specific patterns existed before 1800, are debated. Treat “young” as a fair description of the **system**, not of tartan itself.
