When a young woman married into a traditional Kazakh family, she entered a house with several generations under one roof. Among the rules she learned was **at tergeu**: she **does not pronounce the personal names** of her husband’s elder relatives, his father, mother, older brothers and their wives.

## Inventing new names

She can’t just avoid them, though; she has to call them something. So she gives each one a **new name**: affectionate, respectful, and ideally fitting their personality or role. An older brother-in-law might become something like “the noble one” or “the clever one”. A younger relative might get a playful name.

Doing this well was a **test**. A bride whose nicknames were apt, kind and witty showed intelligence and tact, and earned the family’s respect in her first months in the household. Badly chosen names would be remembered too.

## Part of a wider system

At tergeu belongs to a broader etiquette of **respectful avoidance** between a daughter-in-law and her husband’s senior relatives, which in some households also governed where she sat, when she spoke and how she served food. In large families with three or four generations living together, these rules kept relationships formal and peaceful.

## The naming puzzle

Here is a consequence that many Kazakhs mention. If a mother could not say her father-in-law’s name, giving her baby **his name** would mean she could never call her own child. Families often say that this is one reason children were **not named after close ancestors**, traditionally within seven generations, though this explanation is passed on orally more than it is documented.

Today the custom survives mainly in its gentle form: many daughters-in-law still use the nicknames they gave their in-laws decades ago, and children grow up thinking of them as real names.
