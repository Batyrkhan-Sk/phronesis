You have seen the headlines: *Is this the end of the office? Has science found the cure for ageing? Could your coffee be killing you?* **Betteridge’s law of headlines** says to answer them all the same way: **no**.

## Where it came from

In **February 2009** the British technology journalist **Ian Betteridge** read a TechCrunch article whose headline asked whether the music service Last.fm had handed user listening data to the recording industry body, the RIAA. In a blog post he called it a great demonstration of his maxim that *any headline which ends in a question mark can be answered by the word “no”*.

He did not invent the thought. A **1991** book of Murphy’s-law variants lists it as “Davis’s law”, and the broadcaster **Andrew Marr** advised readers of his 2004 book *My Trade* to answer “no” to newspaper questions. Betteridge’s name stuck because he gave it a crisp wording at a moment when online headlines were getting more speculative.

## Why it often works

The reasoning is simple and fair to journalists. If a newspaper *knew* that Last.fm had handed over the data, it would say so. A question lets a writer raise a possibility, and borrow its excitement, without having to prove it. When the evidence is there, the headline tends to be a statement.

## Where it fails

It is a rule of thumb, not a law:

- Some question headlines really are answered “yes”.
- Questions starting with *who*, *what* or *why* cannot be answered yes or no, so the rule does not apply.
- A question can be an honest way to frame an explainer, as in “How does a vaccine work?”.

The useful habit is not to dismiss every question, but to notice what the headline is *not* telling you. Ask: if the answer were yes, what would the writer have been able to say outright?

## A kind of claim laundering

Seen this way, the question mark does a job similar to “allegedly” or “critics say”: it lets a claim appear in public while the writer keeps some distance from it. The rule is therefore best read as a prompt to look for the evidence in the article itself. If the body of the story can’t answer the question the headline posed, the answer was probably “no” all along, or at best “we don’t know yet”.
