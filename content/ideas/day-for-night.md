Real night is hard to film. It is dark, so you need a great deal of light, and the cast and crew must work through the small hours. For much of film history there was an easier route: shoot in the **daytime** and make it look like night. This is **day for night**.

## How it is done

The basic recipe is to **underexpose** the picture by a few stops, so the scene looks dim. Common tools include:

- a **neutral-density filter** over the lens, which cuts the light;
- a **graduated filter** or polariser to darken the sky;
- in **black-and-white**, a **red filter**, which turns a blue sky almost black;
- colour film balanced for indoor (tungsten) light, which gives a cool blue cast in daylight, or colour grading in post-production today.

## Why “American night”?

In French the technique is **la nuit américaine**. One explanation is that American films, Westerns especially, used it so often. That is the common reading rather than a documented decision by any one person.

**François Truffaut** borrowed the phrase for his 1973 film *Day for Night* (*La Nuit américaine*), set during the making of a melodrama. In it a director explains the trick, and the film is as much about the magic of making films as about deception.

## Spotting it

Next time you watch an older film, look for clues:

1. **Hard shadows** with sharp edges, which moonlight rarely casts.
2. A **sky that is too bright** or an unnaturally dark one, with no stars.
3. **Distant detail** that is far too clear for midnight.
4. Characters who can see everything without a single lamp.

None of these proves it on its own, but together they are a good hint. It is a small piece of film literacy that makes you better company at the cinema, and a gentle way to appreciate how much craft goes into making you believe it is dark.
