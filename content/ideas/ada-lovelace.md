**Augusta Ada King, Countess of Lovelace** (1815–1852) was the only legitimate child of the poet Lord Byron. Her mother, wary of anything that resembled Byron’s temperament, had her tutored intensively in mathematics.

At 17 she met **Charles Babbage**, who was designing a mechanical general-purpose computer, the [Analytical Engine](idea:analytical-engine). In 1843 she translated an Italian engineer’s article about it and added her own **notes, which ended up roughly three times longer than the article**.

## What she wrote

**Note G** sets out, step by step, how the engine could compute **Bernoulli numbers**: which operations to perform, in what order, and which variables to store. It is often called the first published computer program.

She also took the idea further than its inventor. The engine, she wrote, “might act upon other things besides number”. If musical notes could be represented symbolically, it “might compose elaborate and scientific pieces of music”. That is the leap from calculator to computer.

## The debate about “first”

Historians disagree on how to credit her. Babbage had written sequences of operations for the engine before 1843, though they were never published. Some scholars argue Lovelace mainly expanded on his work. Others, pointing to her letters, argue the conceptual insight in the notes was hers.

The fairest summary is that she wrote **the first published, detailed program** for a general-purpose machine, and **the clearest early vision of what computers could become**. The engine was never built, so the program never ran. In 1980 the US Department of Defense named its programming language **Ada** in her honour.
