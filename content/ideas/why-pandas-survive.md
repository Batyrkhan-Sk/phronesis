On paper the giant panda shouldn’t work. It’s a **bear**, in the order **Carnivora**, with the short, simple gut of a meat-eater and few genes for breaking down plant fibre. Yet about **99%** of what it eats is **bamboo**, which is tough and low in nutrients.

So how has it survived for millions of years?

## 1. Eat almost all day

A panda eats about **9–14 kg** of bamboo a day and spends much of the day feeding. Because it digests so little, food passes through quickly; pandas can defecate up to **40 times a day**.

## 2. Spend almost nothing

In **2015**, researchers measured wild and captive pandas with tracking collars and isotope methods. Their **daily energy expenditure** was only about **38%** of what you’d expect for a mammal their size, comparable to a **sloth**.

How?

- **Moving little and slowly**, resting a lot.
- **Smaller vital organs** (brain, liver, kidneys) than expected for their body size.
- **Low thyroid hormone levels**, linked to a mutation in a gene unique to pandas, which slows the whole metabolism.

What looks like laziness is a strategy.

## 3. Few competitors, few enemies

Bamboo forests are **abundant**, and few other large animals compete for bamboo. An adult panda weighs around 100 kg and has a **very powerful bite**, built for crushing bamboo, so adults have few natural predators. Small cubs are far more vulnerable.

The panda isn’t a predator, but it isn’t defenceless either. It’s a heavily built bear that has specialised in a food almost nobody else wants.

## A taste for meat, lost

Pandas carry broken versions of **TAS1R1**, a gene for the **umami** taste receptor that makes meat taste savoury. Scientists think this loss probably happened **after** pandas started eating more bamboo: once meat mattered less, there was no pressure to keep the receptor working. Whether the loss helped lock pandas into bamboo is still debated.

They haven’t entirely given up meat, though: wild pandas occasionally eat small animals or carrion.

## Back from the brink

The 2014 national survey counted about **1,864** wild pandas, and today’s estimate is about **1,900**. In **2016** the IUCN downgraded the species from **Endangered** to **Vulnerable**, mainly because of protected reserves and replanted forest. The low-energy lifestyle has a cost, though: pandas need large areas of continuous bamboo forest, so fragmented habitat is still their main threat.
