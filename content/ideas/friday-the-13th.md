Every year brings at least one Friday the 13th, and with it the same explanation: “It goes back to the Knights Templar.” It’s a good story. It is also not supported by evidence.

## Two fears, not one

Friday has long been considered an unlucky day in some traditions, and 13 an unlucky number. But the **combination** is a different matter. Researchers who have looked for it find the earliest written references to Friday the 13th as unlucky in **nineteenth-century France**, in writing and plays. Snopes and others note that the two fears seem to have merged only around the **turn of the twentieth century**.

## An early popular boost

In 1907 **Thomas Lawson** published a novel called *Friday, the Thirteenth*. It is cited as one of the early popular references, a sign that the idea was well enough known to sell a book.

## The Templar story

The tale says the superstition began with the arrest of the **Knights Templar**. Experts say there is no evidence connecting that event to the superstition, and modern popular fiction helped spread the link. We label it a **myth**.

## What to say instead

You don’t need to spoil anyone’s Friday. Just offer the more interesting fact: the fear is younger than the legends make it sound, and it was probably assembled from two older ones.

## Why it persists

Once a day has a reputation, mishaps on it get noticed and remembered while ordinary days pass unremarked. That is what the [frequency illusion](idea:frequency-illusion) describes, and it keeps the superstition alive without any need for an ancient origin.
