The most famous tilt in the world was never planned. Work on the bell tower of Pisa’s cathedral began in **1173**, and it started to lean while still being built, because the ground beneath is soft.

## A lucky delay

Construction went on, with long interruptions, for around two hundred years. Those gaps probably helped: while the builders were away, the soil under the tower had time to settle and gain strength, so it did not collapse as it might have under continuous building. That is an interpretation, but a widely held one.

## The rescue

By the late twentieth century the lean was still growing and the tower was **closed to visitors in 1990**. The engineers, led by **John Burland** among others, tried several things:

- **Lead weights** placed on the high side in the 1990s, as a temporary measure.
- **Soil extraction**: carefully removing small amounts of soil from under the high side, so the tower slowly settled back.

The tower reopened in **December 2001**. The lean was reduced by roughly **half a metre** at the top (sources quote about 45–50 cm), not enough to look straight, which was deliberate: the aim was to make it safe, and keep what visitors come to see.

## What Galileo didn’t do

You will often hear that **Galileo** dropped two balls from the tower to prove that heavy and light objects fall together. The only source that places such an experiment at the tower is a biography by his pupil Vincenzo Viviani, written decades later. Galileo himself never describes it. A comparable experiment is documented in **1586**, when Simon Stevin and Jan Cornets de Groot dropped lead balls from a church tower in **Delft**.

## Reading the figures

Dates and amounts differ between sources: lead weights are dated to 1993–1994 in one account, and soil extraction to 1999 or 2000. Quote the overall picture (closed 1990, reopened 2001, straightened by about half a metre) rather than a precise date for each step.
