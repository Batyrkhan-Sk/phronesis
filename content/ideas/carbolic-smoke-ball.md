In November 1891, with influenza spreading across Britain, the **Carbolic Smoke Ball Company** ran newspaper adverts for a rubber ball filled with carbolic acid powder, which users were meant to sniff through the nose. The advert made a bold promise: **£100** to anyone who used the ball three times a day for two weeks and still caught influenza. To show it was serious, the company said it had put **£1,000** in the Alliance Bank.

## The customer who took it at its word

**Louisa Carlill** bought a ball and used it as directed from late November. On **17 January 1892** she caught influenza. She asked for her £100 and the company refused, so she sued.

## What the company argued

The company’s position was essentially that nobody could really have meant the advert. It was **“puffery”**: the sort of exaggerated boast every advertiser makes, not a promise a court should enforce. The judge disagreed, and so did the **Court of Appeal**, which ruled unanimously for Mrs Carlill.

The key was the **£1,000 deposit**. A company that sets aside money to pay claims has told you, in the judges’ view, that it intends to be bound. The advert was therefore a real **offer to the whole world**, and anyone who did what it asked had **accepted** it. No one had to write back first.

## Why lawyers still teach it

The case is a classic for two reasons. It shows how a **unilateral contract** works: one side promises something in return for an act, and the contract exists the moment the act is done. And it draws a useful line between a **boast** (“the best coffee in town”) and a **specific promise with conditions and money behind it**.

The next time an advert offers a refund, a guarantee or a prize, the 1892 reasoning is a helpful way to tell which kind it is.
