In the summer of 1977, Ohio State University’s **Big Ear** radio telescope was part of one of the first long-running searches for signals from other civilisations. It could not steer. It sat still while the Earth’s rotation swept each patch of sky across its beam, so any given source was in view for only about **72 seconds**.

On **15 August 1977** it recorded something unusual: a strong, **narrowband** signal, roughly thirty times louder than the background noise around it, near the frequency of the **hydrogen line** (about 1420 MHz). It came from the direction of **Sagittarius**.

## Why “Wow!”

A few days later, astronomer **Jerry Ehman** was going through the computer printouts and spotted the sequence **6EQUJ5**. He circled it in red and wrote “Wow!” in the margin. The name stuck.

The characters are not a coded message. The printout recorded signal strength in a compact scheme: digits for weak readings, then letters as the intensity climbed. Read left to right, the string is simply the signal getting stronger, peaking, and fading as the sky drifted through the beam.

## Why it matters, and why it is still unsolved

The hydrogen line is the most abundant element’s natural radio frequency, and astronomers have long suggested that a civilisation hoping to be noticed might choose it. That is an **interpretation**, not evidence of intent.

What makes the signal famous is that it never came back. Follow-up observations of the same region, by Ehman and many others, found nothing. A signal that cannot be repeated cannot be checked, so science has no way to settle what it was.

In 2017 a researcher proposed that hydrogen clouds around passing comets could explain it. That idea has not been accepted by the wider community, partly because the comets were not in the right place at the time.

## Something to say at dinner

The honest answer to “was it aliens?” is: **we do not know, and we probably cannot find out**. That is a better story than either “yes” or “no”. It is the best-known example of a real observation that science can describe but not explain.
