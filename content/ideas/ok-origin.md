In the late 1830s, young Boston journalists had a craze for **comic abbreviations of misspelled phrases**: “K.Y.” for *know yuse* (no use), “O.W.” for *oll wright* (all right), “N.S.” for *nuff said*. It was the 19th-century version of internet slang.

On 23 March 1839, the *Boston Morning Post* printed “o.k.” for *oll korrect*. Most of these jokes disappeared within months. OK should have too.

## The campaign that saved it

In 1840, President Martin Van Buren ran for re-election. He was from Kinderhook, New York, and his supporters called him **“Old Kinderhook”** and formed “O.K. Clubs”. The slogan worked twice: it was his nickname and it meant “all correct”. Van Buren lost, but the word had spread across the country.

## Why it lasted

OK is short, easy to say in almost any language, and hard to mishear. When telegraph operators needed a quick way to say “received, all good”, OK was ready. Today it is probably the most widely understood expression on Earth. It was even among the first words spoken on the Moon, when Buzz Aldrin said “OK, engine stop” just after landing.

## How the mystery was solved

For a century, nobody knew where OK came from. Popular theories pointed to Choctaw *okeh*, to a biscuit brand, to a railway clerk named Obadiah Kelly, and to Andrew Jackson supposedly misspelling “all correct”.

The Columbia linguist **Allen Walker Read** settled it in a series of articles in *American Speech* in 1963–64. He went through 1830s newspapers page by page and found the abbreviation craze, the 1839 first use and the 1840 campaign. His explanation is now the scholarly consensus. Allan Metcalf’s 2011 book tells the whole story.

The rival etymologies are good examples of how a word with an obscure origin collects colourful stories. People find a dull explanation (“newspaper in-joke”) less satisfying than an exotic one.
