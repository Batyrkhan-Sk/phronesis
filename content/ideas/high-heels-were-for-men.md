Today heels mean glamour, usually women’s. For much of their history they meant something quite different: **horses, war and rank**.

## Built for the saddle

The most common explanation is that heels began as **riding wear in Persia**. A raised heel helps a rider hold the foot firmly in a stirrup, which matters for a mounted archer shooting while standing in the saddle. This is a sensible interpretation, and many histories repeat it, but the early evidence is thin, so it’s best told as “probably”.

## A fashion for noblemen

By the 1600s heeled shoes and boots were in fashion in European courts, and they were worn by men. A heel showed you didn’t have to walk through mud: you rode, or were carried.

**Louis XIV** is the best-known wearer. He is famous for his red-heeled shoes, and red heels came to be associated with his court. You will often read that he issued a decree reserving red heels for nobles. Reports of the date and exact wording differ, so it’s safer to say that red heels were a court status symbol.

You will also read that **Persian envoys** brought heels to Europe at the turn of the 17th century. It’s a popular story, but the link is not securely documented.

## How heels changed sides

In the 18th century men’s fashion turned towards plainer, flatter styles, and heels became mainly women’s wear, a split that has stayed with us. Male heels never entirely disappeared, though: cowboy and riding boots still have them for the old reason.

## A good thing to bring to dinner

It’s a gentle reminder that fashion codes feel eternal and are not. What looks “obviously feminine” now was a symbol of male power not very long ago.
