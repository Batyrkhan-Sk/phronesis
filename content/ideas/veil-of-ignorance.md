Imagine you are asked to write the rules of a society: its laws, schools, taxes and healthcare. There is one condition. You will be born into it, but you will not know **who** you will be: rich or poor, healthy or ill, clever or not, any religion, any gender.

This is the **veil of ignorance**, a thought experiment from the philosopher **John Rawls** in *A Theory of Justice* (1971). The place behind the veil is his **original position**.

## What the veil does

Normally we argue for rules that suit our own situation. The veil removes that bias. Not knowing where you will land, you have a reason to want a system that is **fair to everyone**, because you might be anyone.

Rawls argued that people there would choose equal basic liberties, and would accept inequalities only if they work to the benefit of the **least advantaged**. It is a bit like cutting a cake when you will not know which slice is yours: you cut evenly.

## Not only Rawls

The economist **John Harsanyi** used a similar equal-chance idea in the 1950s. The two reached different conclusions. Harsanyi imagined being equally likely to be any person and concluded that average welfare should be maximised, whereas Rawls’s deeper veil led to protecting the worst-off. So the device is not a machine that gives one answer.

## The arguments

Philosophers still disagree. Would real people be that cautious about the risk of ending up poor, or would some gamble? Does hiding everything about yourself leave anyone with views at all? These are live debates, so it is fairest to describe the veil as a **test**, not a proof.

## Using it at dinner

You do not need the full theory. When a rule, a policy or a family arrangement is argued about, ask: *would I still defend this if I did not know which person in the story I would be?* It is a gentle, friendly question, and it often shifts a conversation from winning to understanding.
