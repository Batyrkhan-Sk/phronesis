**Edward Bulwer-Lytton** (1803–1873) was one of the most popular British novelists of his day, and he left two very different marks on English.

## The line that stuck for good reasons

In his play *Richelieu; or the Conspiracy*, first staged at London’s Covent Garden on **7 March 1839**, the cardinal says that the pen is mightier than the sword. Bulwer-Lytton did not invent the thought: **George Whetstone** had written something similar in **1582**. But the compact wording we use is his.

## The line that stuck for bad ones

Nine years earlier, in his novel *Paul Clifford* (1830), he began a chapter with **“It was a dark and stormy night; the rain fell in torrents…”** The sentence carries on, with a gust of wind sweeping the London streets and bothering the lamps, and is a classic example of an opening that tries too hard.

Fairness matters here. The phrase “dark and stormy night” was already around, and many commentators argue that it was a well-worn cliché even in his day. He did not coin it; he simply gave it its most famous home.

## How a cliché became a prize

In **1965** the cartoonist Charles Schulz had Snoopy start a novel with the line in *Peanuts*, and generations of readers learned it as the standard joke about bad fiction. In **1982**, the English department at San Jose State University launched the **Bulwer-Lytton Fiction Contest**, inviting entrants to write the opening sentence of “the worst of all possible novels”. It is still running, and it has spawned categories ranging from science fiction to vile puns.

## Is it fair?

Whether Bulwer-Lytton was truly a poor writer is a matter of taste, not fact. He was hugely successful in his lifetime. A generous way to tell the story: he wrote one line people use seriously and one they use as a joke, and both are still in circulation nearly two centuries on.
