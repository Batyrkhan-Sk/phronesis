On Monday **21 August 1911**, when the Louvre was closed, **Vincenzo Peruggia**, an Italian handyman who had once worked at the museum fitting protective glass, took the painting off the wall, removed its frame in a stairwell, and walked out with it under his workman’s smock.

Nobody noticed for **more than a day**. Staff assumed it was being photographed.

## A global story

The theft was front-page news around the world. When the Louvre reopened, **crowds queued to look at the empty wall** and the four iron hooks. The poet **Guillaume Apollinaire** was arrested, and his friend **Pablo Picasso** was questioned, because of an earlier, unrelated case of statuettes stolen from the museum. Both were cleared.

## The return

For **more than two years** Peruggia kept the painting in his Paris room. In December 1913 he tried to sell it to an art dealer in **Florence**, claiming he wanted to return it to Italy. The dealer called the Uffizi gallery, and Peruggia was arrested. Italy treated him almost as a patriot; he served only a short sentence.

## Why it matters

Before 1911 the Mona Lisa was admired by experts but was not in a class of its own. The theft gave it **a story and a face that everyone recognised**. Fame tends to grow on itself: once something is famous, more people come to see it because it is famous.
