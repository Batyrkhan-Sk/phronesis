Roman engineers built harbours, aqueducts and domes in **concrete** that has outlasted much of what we pour today. How they did it has been argued about for a long time.

## The basic recipe

Roman concrete mixed **lime** with **volcanic ash** (pozzolana) and chunks of rubble. The ash reacts chemically with lime and water, which helps the material set and stay strong, even in damp conditions. That much is well established, and it is why the **Pantheon** and many other buildings still stand. See the [Pantheon’s dome](idea:pantheon-dome) for the best-known example.

## The lumps that looked like mistakes

Under close inspection, Roman concrete is full of small **white lumps of lime**, called lime clasts. For years they were taken as a sign of poor mixing or poor-quality materials.

A **2023 study** published in *Science Advances* examined samples from a city wall at **Privernum** in Italy. The team proposed that the lumps formed because the Romans added **quicklime** directly to the mix, a process called **hot mixing**, which gives off heat. They also suggested the lumps are not flaws at all: when a crack opens and water gets in, the lime in a clast can dissolve and then recrystallise, filling the gap. In laboratory tests, modern mixes made with lime clasts showed this kind of healing.

## How sure should we be?

This is an **interpretation**, and a good one to hold lightly. It is based on particular samples, and it does not overturn the older explanation that volcanic ash and careful building also matter. Other researchers emphasise the ash, and the chemistry of seawater harbour concrete in particular. A fair summary is that lime clasts may well contribute, and that nobody should say they are *the* secret.

That honesty is part of the pleasure. “The Romans were cleverer than we thought” is a good story. “The thing we called a mistake may not have been” is a better one.
