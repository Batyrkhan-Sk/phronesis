Taste buds detect sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami. **Chilli heat isn’t on the list.** Capsaicin binds to **TRPV1**, a receptor on pain-sensing nerves whose usual job is to warn you about **dangerous heat** above about 43 °C.

So when you eat a hot pepper, your nerves report, quite accurately as far as they can tell, that **your mouth is on fire**. That’s why water doesn’t help much (capsaicin is oily) while milk and yoghurt do: the fat and the protein casein help wash it away.

The same family of receptors explains mint: **menthol** triggers **TRPM8**, the cold sensor, which is why mint feels cool.

## Why chillies are hot at all

Mammals tend to chew seeds and destroy them. Birds swallow them whole and drop them far away. Bird TRPV1 receptors **don’t respond to capsaicin**, so birds eat the hottest peppers happily. One leading hypothesis is that capsaicin **deters the wrong seed dispersers** and leaves the right ones alone.

Humans broke the arrangement. We learned to *enjoy* the burn, possibly because of the endorphin response, the thrill of a safe danger, or plain cultural habit, and we carried chillies around the world within a century of 1492.

## A Nobel Prize

**David Julius** used capsaicin to find the TRPV1 receptor. It earned him a share of the **2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine**.
