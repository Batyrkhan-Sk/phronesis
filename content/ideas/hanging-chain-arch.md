Take a chain and hold it by both ends. It sags into a smooth curve. Now imagine freezing that curve and flipping it upside down. You have just designed an arch.

## Why it works

Every link in a hanging chain is pulled along the chain, never pushed or bent. That is what a chain can do. The curve it settles into is called a **catenary**, from the Latin for chain.

Turn it over and every pull becomes a push. Stone and brick are strong when squeezed and weak when stretched or bent, so an arch of this shape keeps all its material in **compression**. That is why such arches can stand without glue or iron.

## Hooke’s riddle

In 1675 the English scientist **Robert Hooke** published a Latin **anagram** in an appendix to a book on telescopes. Anagrams were a common way to claim an idea without giving it away. In 1705, after his death, his executor revealed the solution: *ut pendet continuum flexile, sic stabit contiguum rigidum inversum*. Roughly: as hangs the flexible line, so, inverted, will stand the rigid arch.

## A common mix-up

For a long time the hanging curve was assumed to be a **parabola**. It is not: they look alike near the bottom but differ further out. The true equation was worked out in the 1690s.

## Designing with string

The idea is also a design method. Hang weighted cords from a board, and the forces draw the structure for you; photograph or mirror the result and you have a vault. **Antoni Gaudí** is famous for using hanging models in this spirit, and the attic of Casa Milà in Barcelona is covered by a series of catenary arches. Whether he knew Hooke’s riddle in particular is unclear, and it does not matter: the physics is the same.

## Something to try

Next time you see a chain or a washing line, remember that it is drawing you an arch. Look for the shape in bridges and vaults: the Gateway Arch in St Louis is a famous, slightly flattened relative.
